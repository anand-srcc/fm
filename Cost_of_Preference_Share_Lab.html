Foundations

Cost of Preference Share (Kp) — Introduction

Preference shares are a hybrid security — they have features of both debt and equity. The cost of preference share (Kp) is the minimum rate of return that the firm must earn on the funds raised through preference share capital to maintain the market price of those shares.

Why Kp is unique

  • Fixed dividend: Preference shareholders receive a fixed dividend (unlike equity where it varies). This makes the cash flows more predictable — similar to debt.
  • No tax shield: Unlike debt interest (which is tax-deductible), preference dividends are paid out of after-tax profits. This makes Kp more expensive than after-tax Kd for equivalent gross returns.
  • Priority over equity: In dividends and liquidation, preference holders rank above equity but below debt. This intermediate risk position gives Kp its intermediate cost: Kd (after-tax) < Kp < Ke.
  • Cumulative / Non-cumulative: Unpaid dividends on cumulative preference shares accumulate and must be paid before equity dividends. Non-cumulative unpaid dividends lapse.

Two main types for cost computation

1. Irredeemable (Perpetual) Preference Shares
No fixed maturity date — dividend is paid in perpetuity. Company never repays the principal. Kp = Annual Dividend / Net Proceeds (a simple perpetuity formula).
2. Redeemable Preference Shares
Have a fixed maturity — capital is repaid at redemption value on a specified date. Kp must account for both the dividend stream AND the redemption (capital repayment). Requires either the approximate formula or the IRR/YTM method.

Types of Preference Shares

Cumulative: Unpaid dividends accumulate (arrears) and must be cleared before equity dividends. More investor protection → lower required return.
Redeemable: Repaid at maturity. Finite life — Kp calculation requires handling redemption value. Under Indian Companies Act, most new issues must be redeemable.
Participating: In addition to fixed dividend, may share in surplus profits with equity shareholders.
Non-Cumulative: Unpaid dividends lapse — not carried forward. Less investor protection → higher required return.
Irredeemable (Perpetual): No fixed maturity — dividend paid in perpetuity. Uncommon in modern corporate practice; mostly theoretical in FM.
Convertible: Can be converted into equity shares at a specified date/ratio. Kp computation gets complex (option value).

Preference vs Debenture — Cost comparison

Assume: FV=₹100, Rate=12%, Tax=30% Debenture (Kd after tax): Interest = ₹12 Tax saving = 12 × 0.30 = ₹3.60 Net cost = ₹8.40 Kd = 8.40/100 = 8.40% Preference Share (Kp): Dividend = ₹12 No tax deduction Kp = 12/100 = 12.00% Kp > Kd because preference dividends carry NO tax shield. Difference: 12% − 8.40% = 3.60% = (Dividend rate × Tax rate)
Key exam point: Preference dividend is NOT tax-deductible. There is no (1−Tc) adjustment for Kp, unlike Kd.

Cost Hierarchy — Position of Kp

Kp sits squarely between the after-tax cost of debt (Kd) and the cost of equity (Ke / Kr). This position is determined by the risk and tax treatment of each source.

SourceRisk RankTax BenefitTypical Cost
Debt (Kd after-tax)Lowest — secured, priority claimYes — interest deductibleCheapest
Preference Share (Kp)Medium — priority over equity, fixed dividendNo — dividend not deductibleIntermediate
Retained Earnings (Kr)High — residual claim, no guaranteeNo tax benefitHigh
New Equity (Ke)Highest — residual + flotationNo tax benefit + flotationHighest
Why Kp > Kd (after-tax)?
Both debenture and preference shareholders receive a fixed return. But:
• Interest on debt is tax-deductible → effective cost = rate × (1−Tc)
• Preference dividend is NOT deductible → effective cost = full rate
Even if both carry the same stated rate, Kp > Kd (after-tax).

Why Kp < Ke?
Preference holders have priority over equity in both dividends and liquidation. They bear less risk than equity shareholders, so they accept a lower return.

Numerical illustration

Firm with: Debt at 12%, Tax 30%; Preference at 12%; Equity Ke = 15%.

Kd (after-tax) = 12% × (1−0.30) = 12% × 0.70 = 8.40% Kp = 12% (no tax adjustment) Ke = 15% Hierarchy: Kd = 8.40% < Kp = 12% < Ke = 15%
Teaching note: The gap between Kd (8.40%) and Kp (12%) is the tax shield: 12%×30% = 3.60%. This is why firms prefer debt to preference shares from a pure cost-of-capital perspective.
Irredeemable Kp

Irredeemable Preference Shares — Concept

An irredeemable (perpetual) preference share pays a fixed dividend forever and is never redeemed. It is a perpetuity. The cost is simply the ratio of the annual dividend to the net proceeds received by the company.

Annual Dividend: PD = Dividend Rate (Dp%) × Face Value (FV) Cost of Irredeemable Preference Share: Kp = PD / NP where: PD = Annual Preference Dividend (₹) NP = Net Proceeds per share (₹) = Issue Price − Flotation Cost = IP − f Special cases: At par with no flotation: NP = FV → Kp = PD/FV = Dp% At premium with no flotation: NP = IP > FV → Kp < Dp% At discount with no flotation: NP = IP < FV → Kp > Dp% With flotation cost: NP = IP − f → Kp rises
Key insight: For irredeemable preference shares, Kp is simply the dividend yield on net proceeds. Because there is no maturity/redemption, there is no capital gain or loss component — the return is entirely from dividends.
Teaching note: Many students confuse face value with net proceeds. Always use NP (= IP − flotation), not FV, unless explicitly told there is no flotation and issued at par. If issued at par with no flotation, NP = FV and Kp = Dp%, but this is a special case.

Comparison of cases

ScenarioNP vs FVKp vs Dp%
At par, no flotationNP = FVKp = Dp%
At premium, no flotationNP > FVKp < Dp%
At discount, no flotationNP < FVKp > Dp%
At par, with flotationNP < FVKp > Dp%
At premium, with flotationNP ≥ or < FVDepends
At discount, with flotationNP < FV (further)Kp >> Dp%
Rule: Anything that reduces NP below FV increases Kp above Dp%. Anything that increases NP above FV reduces Kp below Dp%.
Irredeemable Kp

Irredeemable — Issued at Par

Issued at par means the issue price equals the face value. If there is no flotation cost, NP = FV = IP, and Kp equals the stated dividend rate.

FV = IP = NP (when no flotation cost) PD = Dp% × FV Kp = PD / NP = (Dp% × FV) / FV = Dp% Example: FV=₹100, Dp=12%, No flotation PD = 12% × 100 = ₹12 NP = ₹100 Kp = 12/100 = 12.00% = Dp% With flotation cost ₹5: NP = 100 − 5 = ₹95 Kp = 12/95 = 12.63% > Dp%
Interpretation: When issued at par with no flotation costs, the cost of preference capital equals the stated dividend rate. This is the baseline case. Any deviation (premium, discount, or flotation) changes Kp from Dp%.

Quick illustration

P Ltd. issues 12% preference shares of ₹100 each at par. Compute Kp (a) without flotation cost and (b) with flotation cost ₹6 per share.

PD = 12% × ₹100 = ₹12 (a) No flotation cost: NP = ₹100 Kp = 12/100 = 12.00% (b) Flotation cost ₹6: NP = 100 − 6 = ₹94 Kp = 12/94 = 12.77% Flotation premium = 12.77% − 12.00% = 0.77%
Teaching note: Emphasise that even though the share is issued at par (no premium or discount on price), flotation costs still make Kp exceed Dp%. The market pays ₹100, but the company only keeps ₹94.
Irredeemable Kp

Irredeemable — Issued at Premium

Issued at premium means the issue price exceeds the face value (IP > FV). Higher proceeds for the same dividend → lower cost.

IP > FV (issued at premium) NP = IP − Flotation Cost PD = Dp% × FV (dividend on Face Value, not IP) Kp = PD / NP Note: Dividend is always computed on FACE VALUE, not on the issue price or premium. Example: FV=₹100, IP=₹110, Dp=12%, Flotation=₹4 PD = 12% × 100 = ₹12 NP = 110 − 4 = ₹106 Kp = 12/106 = 11.32% < Dp% of 12% Without flotation: NP = ₹110 Kp = 12/110 = 10.91% << Dp%
Why Kp < Dp% at premium?
The company raises more funds per share (₹110) but pays the same fixed dividend (₹12 on FV ₹100). The effective yield on the funds raised is lower — good for the company, lower cost of capital.

Irredeemable Kp

Irredeemable — Issued at Discount

Issued at discount means the issue price is below face value (IP < FV). Lower proceeds for the same dividend → higher cost. Rarer in practice (regulatory constraints in India).

IP < FV (issued at discount) NP = IP − Flotation Cost (if any) PD = Dp% × FV (always on face value) Kp = PD / NP (NP < FV → Kp > Dp%) Example: FV=₹100, IP=₹90, Dp=12%, No flotation PD = 12% × 100 = ₹12 NP = ₹90 Kp = 12/90 = 13.33% > Dp% of 12% With flotation ₹5: NP = 90 − 5 = ₹85 Kp = 12/85 = 14.12%
Why Kp > Dp% at discount?
The company raises fewer funds per share (₹90) but pays the same fixed dividend (₹12 on FV ₹100). The effective yield on the reduced funds raised is higher — bad for the company, more expensive capital.
Regulatory note (India): Section 53 of the Companies Act 2013 prohibits companies from issuing shares at a discount (applicable to equity). Preference shares can also face restrictions. In practice, this case is more theoretical for exam purposes.

Irredeemable Kp

Flotation Costs — Effect on Irredeemable Kp

Flotation costs (underwriting fees, brokerage, listing charges) reduce the net proceeds from the issue, raising the effective cost of preference capital above the dividend yield on issue price.

NP = IP − f (f = flotation cost per share) = IP × (1 − f%) (f% = flotation as % of IP) Kp = PD / NP Effect of flotation: Without flotation: Kp = PD/IP With flotation: Kp = PD/(IP−f) > PD/IP Flotation premium = Kp(with) − Kp(without) = PD×f / [IP×(IP−f)]

Comprehensive illustration

FV=₹100, IP=₹105, Dp=14%, Flotation = 4% of issue price.

PD = 14% × 100 = ₹14 Flotation (₹) = 4% × 105 = ₹4.20 NP = 105 − 4.20 = ₹100.80 Kp = 14 / 100.80 = 13.89% Without flotation: Kp = 14/105 = 13.33% Flotation premium = 13.89% − 13.33% = 0.56% Interpretation: Flotation costs add 0.56 percentage points to the cost of preference capital.

Two ways to express flotation cost

Flotation stated asFormula for NPExample (IP=₹100)
₹ per share (absolute)NP = IP − ff=₹5 → NP=₹95
% of issue priceNP = IP×(1−f%)f=5% → NP=₹95

Always identify:
1. Is the flotation cost given as ₹ per share or as a percentage?
2. Is the percentage of issue price or face value?
3. Subtract from issue price to get NP, then compute Kp = PD/NP.
Redeemable Kp

Redeemable Preference Shares — Concept

Redeemable preference shares have a fixed maturity. The investor receives annual dividends during the life of the share AND gets back the redemption value (RV) at maturity. Kp must account for both streams — it is the IRR of the cash flows.

Cash flows from the company's perspective (outflows): Year 0: Receive NP (inflow) Year 1 to n: Pay PD (annual dividend — outflow) Year n: Pay RV (redemption value — outflow) Kp is the rate that equates: NP = PD/(1+Kp) + PD/(1+Kp)² + … + PD/(1+Kp)ⁿ + RV/(1+Kp)ⁿ = PD × PVIFA(Kp,n) + RV × PVIF(Kp,n) This is the EXACT method — requires trial and error (IRR). The APPROXIMATE method provides a quick estimate without present value tables.

Three key values

NP — Net Proceeds
What the company receives when the share is issued.
NP = Issue Price − Flotation Cost
PD — Annual Dividend
The fixed annual cash outflow to preference holders.
PD = Dp% × Face Value
RV — Redemption Value
Amount repaid at maturity.
RV = Face Value (at par) or specified amount
When RV ≠ NP: If the company raises less than it repays (NP < RV), the difference is an additional cost spread over n years. If it raises more than it repays (NP > RV), there is a saving — reducing Kp. The approximate method captures this via a simple average.

Redemption value cases

RV relative to FVTypeEffect on Kp
RV = FVRedeemed at parNeutral
RV > FVRedeemed at premiumKp rises (extra cost at end)
RV < FVRedeemed at discountKp falls (saving at end)
NP vs RV:
The capital gain/loss component of Kp for redeemable shares = (RV − NP)/n (spread over life). If RV > NP: additional cost (like buying at discount). If RV < NP: saving (like buying at premium).
Redeemable Kp

Approximate Method — Redeemable Kp

The approximate formula gives a quick, fairly accurate estimate of Kp without using present value tables. It treats the annualised capital gain/loss and dividend together, divided by the average investment.

PD + (RV − NP) / n Kp = ───────────────────────── (NP + RV) / 2 where: PD = Annual Preference Dividend = Dp% × FV RV = Redemption Value NP = Net Proceeds = Issue Price − Flotation Cost n = Life of preference share (years) Numerator: PD + (RV−NP)/n = Annual dividend + Annualised capital gain (or loss) Denominator: (NP+RV)/2 = Average of opening investment (NP) and closing obligation (RV) — approximate average book value The formula is analogous to Kd approximate formula.

Illustration

FV=₹100, Dp=13%, IP=₹98, Flotation=₹3, RV=₹105, n=7 years.

PD = 13% × 100 = ₹13 NP = 98 − 3 = ₹95 RV = ₹105 Capital component = (105 − 95)/7 = 10/7 = ₹1.429/yr Numerator = 13 + 1.429 = ₹14.429 Denominator = (95 + 105)/2 = ₹100 Kp (Approx) = 14.429 / 100 = 14.43%

When capital component is negative

If NP > RV, the company raises more than it repays — this reduces Kp.

Example: NP=₹108, RV=₹100, PD=₹11, n=6 Capital component = (100−108)/6 = −8/6 = −₹1.333/yr Numerator = 11 + (−1.333) = ₹9.667 Denominator = (108+100)/2 = ₹104 Kp (Approx) = 9.667/104 = 9.30%
Accuracy of approximate method:
The approximate method gives a result close to the IRR method but not exact. It is acceptable in exams unless specifically asked for the "exact" or "IRR" method. The YTM method (next page) gives the precise answer.
Memory trick: Kp approx formula = same as Kd approx formula. Both are:
(Annual payment + Annual capital change) / Average of opening and closing values
Redeemable Kp

YTM / IRR Method — Exact Kp

The exact method computes Kp as the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) of the actual cash flows — net proceeds in, dividends and redemption value out. Requires interpolation between two trial rates.

Step 1: List cash flows (from company's perspective) Year 0: +NP (inflow — proceeds received) Year 1 to n: −PD (annual dividend outflow) Year n: −RV (redemption outflow, additional to Year n PD) Step 2: Try Rate r₁ → PV of outflows (PD annuity + RV) PV₁ = PD × PVIFA(r₁,n) + RV × PVIF(r₁,n) Step 3: Try Rate r₂ → PV of outflows PV₂ = PD × PVIFA(r₂,n) + RV × PVIF(r₂,n) Step 4: Interpolate Kp = r₁ + [(NP − PV₁)/(PV₂ − PV₁)] × (r₂ − r₁) Choose r₁ < Kp (so PV₁ > NP) and r₂ > Kp (PV₂ < NP) Bracket Kp between r₁ and r₂ for accurate interpolation.

Full worked illustration

FV=₹100, Dp=12%, IP=₹97, Flotation=₹2, RV=₹102, n=5 years.

PD = 12% × 100 = ₹12 | NP = 97−2 = ₹95 Try r₁ = 14%: PV = 12×PVIFA(14%,5) + 102×PVIF(14%,5) = 12×3.433 + 102×0.519 = 41.196 + 52.938 = ₹94.134 Try r₂ = 13%: PV = 12×PVIFA(13%,5) + 102×PVIF(13%,5) = 12×3.517 + 102×0.543 = 42.204 + 55.386 = ₹97.590 Interpolation (NP = ₹95; PV₁=94.134 at 14%; PV₂=97.590 at 13%): Kp = 14% + [(95−94.134)/(97.590−94.134)] × (14%−13%) Note: r₁=14% gives lower PV; r₂=13% gives higher PV. Kp = 13% + [(97.590−95)/(97.590−94.134)] × 1% = 13% + [2.590/3.456] × 1% = 13% + 0.75% = 13.75%

Interpolation — visual

Rate | PV of outflows ───────────────────── 13% | ₹97.590 ← above NP = ₹95 ?% Kp | ₹95.000 ← NP (breakeven) 14% | ₹94.134 ← below NP = ₹95 Kp lies between 13% and 14% Linear interpolation: Kp = 13 + (97.590−95)/(97.590−94.134) = 13 + 2.590/3.456 = 13.75%
Choosing trial rates:
Start with the approximate Kp as your first guess. Then try rates just above and below until you bracket NP. Use rates that are 1–2% apart for accuracy.

The calculator on the next page solves this exactly using numerical bisection.
Teaching note: Walk students through why PV falls as rate rises (inverse relationship). Higher discount rate → lower present value of future cash flows → PV < NP → the rate is too high. Lower rate → PV > NP → rate too low. Kp is found where PV = NP exactly.

Key observations from the table:
1. Higher issue price (lower NP) → higher Kp — more capital gain at redemption.

3. Flotation costs always increase Kp — they reduce NP without reducing repayment.
4. Longer tenure spreads the capital gain/loss component, affecting Kp.
Special Topics

Dividend Tax Treatment — Why No Tax Adjustment in Kp

This is one of the most critical — and most commonly tested — distinctions in cost of capital theory.

ItemDebt InterestPreference Dividend
PaymentInterest — contractual obligationDividend — discretionary (but practically fixed for pref.)
Tax treatment for companyTax-deductible (Income Tax Act, India)NOT tax-deductible — paid from after-tax profits
Cost formulaKd = Interest×(1−Tc)/NPKp = PD/NP (no tax adjustment)
Effect of corporate tax rateHigher Tc → lower Kd (after-tax)Tc has no impact on Kp
Where paid fromPre-tax income (reduces taxable income)Post-tax income (profits after tax)
Comparison: ₹100 FV, 12% rate, Tax rate 30% Debenture (Kd after-tax): Interest outflow = ₹12 Tax saving = 12 × 30% = ₹3.60 Effective annual cost = ₹8.40 Kd = 8.40/100 = 8.40% ← reduced by tax Preference Share (Kp): Dividend outflow = ₹12 Tax saving = ZERO Effective annual cost = ₹12.00 Kp = 12/100 = 12.00% ← no tax adjustment Kp − Kd (after-tax) = 12% − 8.40% = 3.60% This gap = Dividend Rate × Tax Rate = 12% × 30%

Tax on Preference Dividend in India

Historical context: India abolished Dividend Distribution Tax (DDT) in 2020. Prior to that, companies paid DDT on dividends (including preference dividends) at a flat rate before distribution. Since FY2020-21, dividends are taxable in the hands of the investor at their income tax slab rate. However:

• The dividend is still paid from AFTER-TAX PROFITS of the company
• The company gets NO deduction for preference dividend in computing its taxable income
• Therefore Kp formula has NO (1−Tc) adjustment — dividend tax changes investor returns, not company cost
Exam rule: Never multiply Kp by (1−Tc). Only Kd gets the tax adjustment. This is the most common error students make.
Teaching note: Students confuse dividend tax (paid by investor/company) with interest tax deduction (received by company). Draw a clear P&L line: interest reduces taxable income (before tax); dividends come out of net profit (after tax). Taxability in investor hands is a separate matter.
Special Topics

Kp in the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

Kp enters the WACC as the cost assigned to the preference share component, weighted by its proportion in the overall capital structure.

WACC = Kd×Wd + Kp×Wp + Ke×We where: Wd = Weight of Debt = D / (D+P+E) Wp = Weight of Preference = P / (D+P+E) We = Weight of Equity = E / (D+P+E) Weights can be: Book Value weights: use balance sheet values Market Value weights: use market prices × quantities Target weights: use firm's desired capital structure

Comprehensive WACC illustration

Capital structure: Debt ₹40 lakh (Kd after-tax 8%), Preference ₹20 lakh (Kp 12%), Equity ₹60 lakh (Ke 16%). Total = ₹1,20,00,000.

Total = 40+20+60 = ₹120 lakh Wd = 40/120 = 0.333 Kd = 8.00% Wp = 20/120 = 0.167 Kp = 12.00% We = 60/120 = 0.500 Ke = 16.00% WACC = 0.333×8.00 + 0.167×12.00 + 0.500×16.00 = 2.664 + 2.004 + 8.000 = 12.668% ≈ 12.67%
Book value vs market value weights:
Book value weights are simpler but may not reflect current market conditions. Market value weights are theoretically superior — they reflect current investor expectations. Most Indian exam problems use book value weights unless specified otherwise.

Formula analogy: The Kp approximate formula is structurally identical to the Kd approximate formula — the only difference is that Kd multiplies the annual payment by (1−Tc), while Kp does not. This is the entire difference between the two costs on an after-tax basis.

Which costs more — Kp or Kd?

Assuming same stated rate: FV=₹100, Rate=12%, Tax=30% Irredeemable, issued at par: Kd = 12×(1−0.30)/100 = 8.40% Kp = 12/100 = 12.00% Difference = 3.60% Irredeemable, issued at discount ₹90: Kd = 12×0.70/90 = 9.33% Kp = 12/90 = 13.33% Difference = 4.00% Rule: Kp > Kd always because the tax shield applies to Kd but not to Kp.
Exception: If corporate tax rate = 0% (loss-making firm or tax-exempt entity), then Kd (after-tax) = Kd (pre-tax) and the gap between Kp and Kd narrows to risk premium only.

Example 1 — Irredeemable at Par, no flotation

A company issues 11% preference shares of ₹100 each at par. No flotation cost. Compute Kp.

PD = 11% × ₹100 = ₹11

NP = ₹100 (at par, no flotation)

Kp = PD/NP = 11/100 = 11.00%

When issued at par with no flotation, Kp = Dp% exactly.

Example 2 — Irredeemable with Premium and Flotation

10% preference shares: FV=₹100, Issue price=₹110, Flotation=₹6. Compute Kp.

PD = 10% × ₹100 = ₹10

NP = 110 − 6 = ₹104

Kp = 10/104 = 9.62%

Despite flotation, premium is large enough to keep Kp below Dp% of 10%.

Example 3 — Redeemable, Approximate Method

12% preference shares: FV=₹100, IP=₹96, Flotation=₹4, RV=₹104, n=8 years. Compute Kp (approximate).

PD = 12% × 100 = ₹12; NP = 96−4 = ₹92; RV = ₹104

Capital component = (104−92)/8 = 12/8 = ₹1.50/yr

Numerator = 12 + 1.50 = ₹13.50

Denominator = (92+104)/2 = 196/2 = ₹98

Kp (approx) = 13.50/98 = 13.78%

Example 4 — Redeemable, YTM / IRR Method

Same as Ex.3: PD=₹12, NP=₹92, RV=₹104, n=8 years. Compute Kp by IRR/interpolation.

Try r=14%: PV = 12×PVIFA(14%,8) + 104×PVIF(14%,8)

= 12×4.639 + 104×0.351 = 55.668 + 36.504 = ₹92.172

Try r=15%: PV = 12×PVIFA(15%,8) + 104×PVIF(15%,8)

= 12×4.487 + 104×0.327 = 53.844 + 34.008 = ₹87.852

NP=₹92 lies between PV at 14% (₹92.172) and 15% (₹87.852):

Kp = 14% + [(92.172−92)/(92.172−87.852)]×1% = 14% + [0.172/4.320]×1% = 14% + 0.04% = 14.04%

IRR method gives 14.04% vs approx 13.78% — close but not identical.

Example 5 — Kp in WACC

Capital: Debt ₹50 lakh (Kd=9% after-tax), Pref. ₹30 lakh (14% pref. shares at par, no flotation), Equity ₹70 lakh (Ke=17%). Compute WACC.

Total = 50+30+70 = ₹150 lakh

Wd=50/150=0.333; Wp=30/150=0.200; We=70/150=0.467

Kp = 14% (at par, no flotation = Dp%)

WACC = 0.333×9 + 0.200×14 + 0.467×17

= 2.997 + 2.800 + 7.939 = 13.74%

Example 6 — Kp vs Kd (No tax shield on Kp)

Company raises: (a) 12% Debentures at par; (b) 12% Preference shares at par. Tax rate 35%. Compute and compare costs.

(a) Kd = 12% × (1−0.35) = 12% × 0.65 = 7.80%

(b) Kp = 12% (no tax adjustment) = 12.00%

Difference = 12.00% − 7.80% = 4.20% = 12% × 35%

The entire difference is the tax shield lost on preference shares. From WACC perspective, preference is significantly more expensive than debt at the same stated rate when tax rates are high.

Formula Bank — Cost of Preference Share

Annual Preference Dividend

PD = Dp% × Face Value (FV) (Dividend always on face value, not issue price)

Net Proceeds

NP = Issue Price (IP) − Flotation Cost (f) = IP × (1 − f%) if flotation is % of IP

Irredeemable Preference Share

Kp = PD / NP (perpetuity formula)

Redeemable — Approximate Method

PD + (RV − NP) / n Kp = ──────────────────────── (NP + RV) / 2 Numerator = Annual dividend + Annualised capital gain/loss Denominator = Average of NP and RV (average investment)

Redeemable — YTM / IRR (Exact) Method

NP = PD × PVIFA(Kp, n) + RV × PVIF(Kp, n) Solve by interpolation: Kp = r₁ + [(NP − PV₁) / (PV₂ − PV₁)] × (r₂ − r₁) where PV₁ at r₁ > NP and PV₂ at r₂ < NP

WACC with Preference

WACC = Kd×Wd + Kp×Wp + Ke×We Wd = D/(D+P+E); Wp = P/(D+P+E); We = E/(D+P+E)

Cost Hierarchy

Kd (after-tax) < Kp < Kr < Ke (new issue) Kp vs Kd: Kp = I/NP; Kd = I×(1−Tc)/NP Gap = I×Tc/NP = Dp% × Tc (at par)

Key rules

1. No tax adjustment on Kp (unlike Kd) 2. Dividend always on Face Value 3. NP = Issue Price − Flotation Cost 4. If NP < RV → capital loss → Kp rises 5. If NP > RV → capital gain → Kp falls 6. Irredeemable: Kp = PD/NP (simple perpetuity)
