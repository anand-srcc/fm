Foundations

Cost of Debt — Introduction

The cost of debt (Kd) is the effective rate the firm pays on its borrowed funds — the yield required by investors who lend money via debentures, bonds, or term loans. It is the cheapest source of long-term finance because interest is tax-deductible, giving debt a tax shield unavailable to preference shares or equity.

Why cost of debt matters

  • It forms the debt component in WACC — always used as the after-tax rate.
  • It is the minimum return borrowed funds must earn to cover the interest burden.
  • Debt is cheap but not free — excessive debt raises financial risk and eventually raises Kd as lenders demand a default premium.
  • Kd is directly reduced by the government via the tax shield — a 10% coupon costs only 7% after tax at a 30% tax rate.

Foundations

The Tax Shield on Debt

Interest on debt is deductible before tax — the government subsidises part of the interest cost. This is the single most important feature of debt financing.

Tax saving from interest = I × Tc After-tax interest cost = I − I×Tc = I(1−Tc) After-tax Kd = Pre-tax Kd × (1 − Tc)

Illustration — ₹10,00,000 of 12% debentures, Tax 30%

Effective interest cost1,20,000 − 36,000 = ₹84,000
After-tax Kd84,000/10,00,000 = 8.4%

Teaching note: The tax shield is why MM-with-tax shows V_L = V_U + Tc×D. Connect Kd to capital structure theory — the same tax benefit that lowers Kd raises firm value.
Irredeemable Debt

Irredeemable (Perpetual) Debt — Concept & Formula

Irredeemable debt carries no obligation to repay the principal — interest is paid forever. The firm's only cash outflow is the periodic interest payment in perpetuity.

The investor receives interest I every year forever → market price P₀ = I / Kd* (a perpetuity) Rearranging: Pre-tax Kd* = I / NP After-tax Kd = I(1−Tc) / NP = Kd* × (1 − Tc) where NP = Issue Price − Flotation Costs

Cash flow timeline

0
Year 0 (firm receives): NP = Issue Price − Flotation Costs
1…∞
Every year forever (firm pays): I(1−Tc) after-tax interest
∞
No redemption — principal never repaid.
Effect of issue price on Kd (Irredeemable):
Issued atNP vs FVKd vs Coupon×(1−Tc)
Par (no flotation)NP = FVKd = Coupon×(1−Tc)
DiscountNP < FVKd > Coupon×(1−Tc)
PremiumNP > FVKd < Coupon×(1−Tc)

Illustrations

Case 1 — At Par: 12% debentures, FV ₹100, issued at par, no flotation. Tax 30%.

NP = ₹100 Kd = 12×0.70/100 = 8.40%

Case 2 — At Discount: 8% debentures, FV ₹1,000, issued ₹920, flotation ₹15. Tax 35%.

NP = 920−15 = ₹905 Kd* = 80/905 = 8.84% Kd = 80×0.65/905 = 52/905 = 5.75%

Case 3 — At Premium: 10% debentures, FV ₹100, issued ₹108, flotation ₹3. Tax 30%.

NP = 108−3 = ₹105 Kd* = 10/105 = 9.52% Kd = 10×0.70/105 = 7/105 = 6.67%

Redeemable Debt

Redeemable Debt — Concept

Redeemable debt has a fixed maturity — the firm pays interest for n years and repays the principal (Redemption Value) at the end.

Cash flow timeline

0
Year 0 (firm receives): NP = Issue Price − Flotation Costs
1..n
Years 1 to n (firm pays): I(1−Tc) after-tax interest each year
n
Year n (firm pays): Redemption Value RV (usually = Face Value)
Key complication vs irredeemable: When issued at discount (NP < RV), the firm repays more than it received → capital loss to firm (= gain to investor) → raises Kd. When issued at premium and redeemed at par (NP > RV), firm repays less → lowers Kd. This capital gain/loss must be incorporated — either via the approximate formula or by exact YTM.

Capital gain / loss effect

SituationRV vs NPEffect on Kd
Issued at par, redeemed at parRV = NPKd = Coupon×(1−Tc)
Issued at discountRV > NPKd rises (capital gain to investor)
Issued at premium, redeemed at parRV < NPKd falls (capital loss to investor)
Redeemed at premiumRV > FV = NPKd rises further

Two methods — when to use which

Approximate Method: Use when the question asks for a quick computation or specifies "approximate" or "short-cut." Fast, direct formula. Slightly overestimates Kd for discount issues. Standard in Indian FM exams.
YTM / IRR Method: Use when the question asks for "exact" cost or "using PV tables." Involves trial rates + linear interpolation. More accurate — especially when the difference between NP and RV is large or when n is long.
Exam tip: If the question provides present value factor tables, use the YTM method. If it asks to "compute Kd" without specifying, the approximate method is usually expected unless explicitly stated otherwise.
Redeemable Debt

Redeemable Kd — Approximate (Short-Cut) Method

After-tax Kd = I(1−Tc) + (RV − NP)/n ───────────────────────── (RV + NP) / 2 Pre-tax Kd* = I + (RV − NP)/n ───────────────────────── (RV + NP) / 2 where: I = Annual interest = Coupon% × FV Tc = Corporate tax rate RV = Redemption Value NP = Net Proceeds = Issue Price − Flotation Cost n = Years to maturity

Understanding each part

Numerator = annual net cost to firm:
I(1−Tc) = after-tax interest paid every year
(RV−NP)/n = annual equivalent of the capital gain/loss at redemption — spread evenly over n years

Denominator = average funds outstanding:
(RV+NP)/2 = average of what was received (NP) and what will be repaid (RV)

Step-by-step illustration

10% debentures, FV ₹1,000, issued ₹950, flotation ₹20, redeemable at par after 5 years. Tax 30%.

Step 1: I = 10% × 1,000 = ₹100 Step 2: I(1−Tc) = 100 × 0.70 = ₹70 Step 3: NP = 950 − 20 = ₹930 Step 4: Annual capital gain = (1,000−930)/5 = 70/5 = ₹14 Step 5: Average funds = (1,000+930)/2 = ₹965 Step 6: Kd = (70+14)/965 = 84/965 = 8.70% Pre-tax Kd* = (100+14)/965 = 114/965 = 11.81%

Redeemable Debt

Redeemable Kd — Exact YTM / IRR Method

The exact Kd is the after-tax YTM (Yield to Maturity) — the discount rate that makes the PV of all future after-tax cash outflows equal to the net proceeds received today.

Find Kd such that: NP = Σ[I(1−Tc)/(1+Kd)ᵗ] + RV/(1+Kd)ⁿ Equivalently, the IRR of the cash flow stream: Year 0: +NP (inflow to firm) Year 1–n: −I(1−Tc) (interest outflow) Year n: −RV (redemption outflow, in addition to interest) Solved by Trial & Error + Linear Interpolation: Choose L (low rate) → NPV_L > 0 Choose H (high rate) → NPV_H < 0 Kd = L + [NPV_L / (NPV_L − NPV_H)] × (H − L)

Full illustration (same data as Approx. example)

10% deb, FV ₹1,000, NP ₹930, RV ₹1,000, n=5, Tax 30%. After-tax interest = ₹70/yr.

Try L = 8%: PV of interest = 70 × PVAF(8%,5) = 70 × 3.993 = 279.51 PV of RV = 1,000 × PVF(8%,5) = 1,000 × 0.681 = 681.00 Total PV = 960.51 NPV_L = 960.51 − 930 = +30.51 ✓ (positive) Try H = 10%: PV of interest = 70 × PVAF(10%,5) = 70 × 3.791 = 265.37 PV of RV = 1,000 × PVF(10%,5) = 1,000 × 0.621 = 621.00 Total PV = 886.37 NPV_H = 886.37 − 930 = −43.63 ✓ (negative) Kd = 8 + [30.51 / (30.51 + 43.63)] × (10 − 8) = 8 + [30.51 / 74.14] × 2 = 8 + 0.411 × 2 = 8 + 0.82 = 8.82% (Approximate method gave 8.70% — close but not exact)

YTM Step-by-Step

1. Identify all after-tax cash flows: NP at Year 0; I(1−Tc) each year; RV at Year n
2. Start with a trial rate slightly below the approximate Kd
3. Compute PV of all outflows at trial rate L → check NPV_L > 0
4. Try a higher rate H → check NPV_H < 0
5. Apply interpolation: Kd = L + [NPV_L/(NPV_L−NPV_H)]×(H−L)
Choosing trial rates: Start with the approximate Kd as a guide — try L = approx Kd − 1% and H = approx Kd + 2%. Adjust if both give the same sign. The approximate method is an excellent starting point for trial rates.
Teaching note: The interpolation formula assumes linearity between the two trial rates — NPV is actually a convex function, so the interpolated result slightly overestimates Kd. The closer L and H, the more accurate the interpolation.

Special Topics

Flotation Costs — Effect on Kd

Flotation costs (issue costs, underwriting fees, brokerage, registration charges) are deducted from the issue price to get the net proceeds (NP). Since Kd = f(1/NP), lower NP means higher Kd.

NP = Issue Price − Flotation Cost (₹ amount) = Issue Price × (1 − f%) if given as percentage Effect on Kd: More flotation cost → Lower NP → Higher Kd For irredeemable: Kd = I(1−Tc) / NP ↑ when NP ↓ For redeemable: Kd = [I(1−Tc)+(RV−NP)/n] / [(RV+NP)/2] Numerator ↑ (RV−NP rises) AND Denominator ↓ (avg falls) → Both effects raise Kd

Illustration — Effect of flotation cost on redeemable Kd

10% deb, FV ₹1,000, issued ₹1,000, redeemable at par, n=5, Tax 30%. I(1−Tc)=₹70.

Flotation (₹)NPNumeratorDenominatorKd
01,00070+0=701,0007.00%
2098070+4=749907.47%
5095070+10=809758.21%
10090070+20=909509.47%
A ₹100 flotation cost on a ₹1,000 debenture raises Kd from 7.00% to 9.47% — a 247 bps increase. This is why firms try to minimise flotation costs and prefer internally generated funds (retained earnings) for equity which carry zero flotation.

Kd rises steeply as flotation costs increase, because they reduce NP while simultaneously raising the annual capital gain component.

Special Topics

Effect of Issue Price on Kd — Full Analysis

The issue price determines the net proceeds and, in the case of redeemable debt, the capital gain or loss at maturity. It is one of the most tested topics in cost of debt problems.

Why issue price matters differently for irredeemable vs redeemable

FactorIrredeemableRedeemable
NP effectYes — directly in denominatorYes — in both numerator and denominator
Capital gain/lossNo — never repaidYes — (RV−NP)/n in numerator
Discount → KdRises (lower NP)Rises more (lower NP + capital gain)
Premium → KdFalls (higher NP)Falls more (higher NP + capital loss)

Sensitivity to issue price — Redeemable 10% Deb, FV ₹1,000, n=5, Tax 30%

Kd falls as issue price rises — both because NP increases and because the capital gain at maturity (RV−NP) decreases.

At Par, Discount, Premium — Summary

Issued at Par (NP = RV):
No capital gain/loss. Kd = Coupon×(1−Tc) exactly (if no flotation). The cleanest case.
Issued at Discount (NP < RV):
Investor gets a capital gain at maturity. This extra return raises the investor's total yield → raises Kd above the after-tax coupon rate. Firm is effectively paying more than the coupon rate.
Issued at Premium (NP > RV = FV):
Investor suffers a capital loss at maturity. This reduces the total return → lowers Kd below the after-tax coupon rate. Firm is effectively paying less than the coupon rate (offset by the higher amount received upfront).
Redeemed at Premium (RV > FV):
Even if issued at par, a redemption premium means RV > NP → capital gain to investor → Kd rises above the after-tax coupon rate.
Special Topics

Cost of Bank Loans / Term Loans

Bank loans (term loans) are the most common form of debt for Indian companies. Unlike debentures, bank loans typically have no issue price variation or flotation costs — they are borrowed and repaid at the same principal amount. The cost computation is simpler.

Case 1 — Simple term loan (bullet repayment)

Pre-tax Kd* = Interest Rate on Loan (%) After-tax Kd = Interest Rate × (1 − Tc) Example: ₹50,00,000 term loan at 12% p.a., Tax 30%. After-tax Kd = 12% × (1 − 0.30) = 8.40% No NP adjustment needed — the firm receives exactly the loan amount and repays exactly the loan amount.

Case 2 — Loan with processing / arrangement fee

If the bank deducts a one-time processing fee (f): NP = Loan Amount − Processing Fee After-tax Kd = Interest(1−Tc) / NP (for perpetual loans) For term loans, use the approximate formula: Kd = [Interest(1−Tc) + (Loan−NP)/n] / [(Loan+NP)/2] Example: ₹10,00,000 loan at 14%, 2% processing fee, 5-year tenor, Tax 30%. NP = 10,00,000 − 20,000 = ₹9,80,000 Interest(1−Tc) = 10,00,000×14%×0.70 = ₹98,000 Capital adjustment = (10,00,000−9,80,000)/5 = ₹4,000 Kd = (98,000+4,000)/[(10,00,000+9,80,000)/2] = 1,02,000/9,90,000 = 10.30%

Bank Loan Kd Calculator

Key difference from debentures: Bank loans have no market price — they are bilateral contracts. There is no YTM to compute in the market sense. The approximate formula (or simply pre-tax rate × (1−Tc) when no fees) is the standard approach.
Kd in WACC

Using Kd in WACC

The after-tax Kd enters WACC as the cost assigned to the debt component, weighted by debt's proportion in the capital structure.

WACC = Wd × Kd(after-tax) + Wp × Kp + We × Ke where Wd = D / (D + P + E) (book or market value weight) Always use AFTER-TAX Kd in WACC — never the pre-tax rate. The tax shield is a real, permanent reduction in the firm's effective cost of borrowing.

Illustration — WACC computation

Capital structure: 12% Debentures ₹30L (NP ₹28L, n=6, RV ₹30L). Preference ₹10L (Kp 11%). Equity ₹60L (Ke 15%). Tax 30%.

Step 1: Compute Kd (approximate) I(1−Tc) = 12%×30L×0.70 = ₹2,52,000 (on FV basis per ₹) Per ₹1,000 FV: I=₹120; I(1−Tc)=₹84; NP=₹933.33; n=6 Kd = [84+(1000−933.33)/6]/[(1000+933.33)/2] = [84+11.11]/966.67 = 95.11/966.67 = 9.84% Step 2: WACC (book value weights) Total = 30+10+60 = ₹100L WACC = (30/100)×9.84% + (10/100)×11% + (60/100)×15% = 2.95% + 1.10% + 9.00% = 13.05%

Common mistakes in using Kd in WACC

1. Using pre-tax Kd: Always multiply by (1−Tc). WACC must use the after-tax cost to reflect the real cash cost to the firm.
2. Using coupon rate directly: The coupon rate only equals Kd when issued at par with no flotation costs. Always compute Kd from NP and RV.
3. Using book value of debt when market value required: If the question asks for MV-WACC, use the current market price of the debenture as the weight, not the face or book value.
Correct approach: Compute after-tax Kd using the appropriate method (approximate or YTM), then use it with the specified weights (book or market value) in the WACC formula.

Example 1 — Irredeemable at Discount

9% irredeemable debentures, FV ₹100, issued at ₹92, flotation ₹4. Tax 35%. Compute pre-tax and after-tax Kd.

I = 9%×100 = ₹9; NP = 92−4 = ₹88

Pre-tax Kd* = 9/88 = 10.23%

After-tax Kd = 9×(1−0.35)/88 = 9×0.65/88 = 5.85/88 = 6.65%

If issued at par (NP=₹100): Kd* = 9%; Kd = 5.85%. Discount raised Kd from 5.85% to 6.65%.

Example 2 — Redeemable, Approximate Method

12% debentures, FV ₹1,000, issued at ₹980, flotation ₹15, redeemable at ₹1,050 after 6 years. Tax 30%. Compute Kd (approximate).

I = 12%×1,000=₹120; I(1−Tc) = 120×0.70=₹84

NP = 980−15 = ₹965; RV = ₹1,050; n = 6

Capital gain = (1,050−965)/6 = 85/6 = ₹14.17

Average funds = (1,050+965)/2 = ₹1,007.50

Kd = (84+14.17)/1,007.50 = 98.17/1,007.50 = 9.74%

Example 3 — YTM / IRR Method (Full Interpolation)

10% debentures, FV ₹1,000, NP ₹930, redeemable at par after 5 years. Tax 30%. Compute exact Kd. PVAF(8%,5)=3.993; PVF(8%,5)=0.681; PVAF(10%,5)=3.791; PVF(10%,5)=0.621.

I(1−Tc) = 100×0.70 = ₹70/yr; RV = ₹1,000

At L = 8%: PV = 70×3.993 + 1000×0.681 = 279.51+681 = 960.51; NPV_L = 960.51−930 = +30.51

At H = 10%: PV = 70×3.791 + 1000×0.621 = 265.37+621 = 886.37; NPV_H = 886.37−930 = −43.63

Kd = 8 + [30.51/(30.51+43.63)]×(10−8) = 8 + (30.51/74.14)×2 = 8 + 0.823 = 8.82%

Example 4 — Redeemable at Premium

11% debentures, FV ₹1,000, issued at par (no flotation), redeemable at ₹1,080 after 7 years. Tax 30%. Compute Kd (approximate).

I = 11%×1,000=₹110; I(1−Tc)=110×0.70=₹77; NP=₹1,000; RV=₹1,080; n=7

Capital gain = (1,080−1,000)/7 = 80/7 = ₹11.43

Avg funds = (1,080+1,000)/2 = ₹1,040

Kd = (77+11.43)/1,040 = 88.43/1,040 = 8.50%

Even though issued at par, the redemption premium raises Kd above 7.7% (= 11%×0.70) because the firm must repay ₹1,080 despite receiving only ₹1,000.

Example 5 — Bank Loan with Processing Fee

₹20,00,000 term loan at 13% per annum. One-time processing fee 1.5%. Loan repaid as bullet after 4 years. Tax 35%. Compute Kd.

Processing fee = 1.5%×20,00,000 = ₹30,000; NP = 20,00,000−30,000 = ₹19,70,000

Annual interest (after-tax) = 13%×20,00,000×(1−0.35) = 2,60,000×0.65 = ₹1,69,000

Capital adjustment = (20,00,000−19,70,000)/4 = 30,000/4 = ₹7,500

Avg funds = (20,00,000+19,70,000)/2 = ₹19,85,000

Kd = (1,69,000+7,500)/19,85,000 = 1,76,500/19,85,000 = 8.89%
