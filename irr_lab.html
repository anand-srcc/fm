Concept & Meaning

Internal Rate of Return — Concept and Meaning

The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes the Net Present Value (NPV) of a project's cash flows exactly equal to zero. In plain terms, it is the effective annual yield the project earns on the capital invested — the break-even rate of return at which the project neither creates nor destroys value.

Core definition: IRR is the rate r that satisfies:
NPV(r) = C₀ + C₁/(1+r)¹ + C₂/(1+r)² + … + Cₙ/(1+r)ⁿ = 0
Equivalently: the rate at which the PV of inflows exactly equals the PV of outflows.

The economic intuition behind IRR

Imagine the project as a special "bank account." You deposit the initial outlay C₀ today, and the account pays back the future cash flows C₁, C₂, …, Cₙ. The IRR is the implicit interest rate that this account earns. If that rate exceeds what you could earn elsewhere at equivalent risk (your WACC), the project adds value; if it falls short, the project destroys value relative to the alternative.

IRR > WACC
Project earns MORE than the cost of capital.
Wealth is CREATED.
ACCEPT
IRR = WACC
Project earns EXACTLY the cost of capital.
Investors fairly compensated.
INDIFFERENT
IRR < WACC
Project earns LESS than the cost of capital.
Wealth is DESTROYED.

Why IRR is so popular (and where it misleads)

  • Intuitive communication: "This project returns 22% against our 12% cost of capital" is far easier for non-finance managers to grasp than "NPV = ₹4.8 lakh."
  • Scale-independent: IRR is a percentage, so it looks comparable across projects of different sizes — but that is exactly where it can mislead (see the scale conflict).
  • No need to specify a discount rate to compute it: the IRR is intrinsic to the cash flows, which is convenient — but it still must be compared to a separately-chosen hurdle rate.
  • Pitfall: Unlike NPV, IRR can give multiple answers, can be undefined, implicitly assumes reinvestment at the IRR, and can rank projects incorrectly for mutually exclusive choices.
Teaching note: Introduce IRR through the bank-account analogy, then immediately contrast with NPV. Ask: "Two projects — A returns 50% on ₹1,000 (₹500 profit); B returns 20% on ₹10,000 (₹2,000 profit). Which is better for the firm?" IRR says A; NPV says B. Plant the seed that IRR is a communication tool, not the final arbiter.

IRR in one sentence

"IRR is the break-even discount rate of a project — the rate at which its NPV is zero — and we accept it only when that rate beats our required return."

Historical note

Like NPV, IRR descends from Irving Fisher's capital theory and was popularised in the mid-20th century. It remains the most widely quoted capital-budgeting metric in corporate practice even though academics favour NPV — which is precisely why understanding its conflicts with NPV is essential.

The IRR Equation — Explained Term by Term

0 = C₀ + C₁/(1+IRR)¹ + C₂/(1+IRR)² + … + Cₙ/(1+IRR)ⁿ Where: C₀ = Initial investment (usually negative: the outlay at Year 0) Cₜ = Net cash flow in year t (positive = inflow, negative = outflow) IRR = The unknown rate that solves the equation n = Project life Solving for IRR is "finding the root" of the NPV function: Define f(r) = NPV(r). We seek r such that f(r) = 0. The IRR is the x-intercept of the NPV-vs-rate curve.

Why there is no closed-form formula

For a single cash flow (lump sum) or a level annuity, the IRR can be isolated algebraically. But for general uneven cash flows the NPV equation is a polynomial of degree n in (1+IRR), and there is no general algebraic solution for degree ≥ 5. So IRR is almost always found by numerical methods: trial-and-error with interpolation, bisection, the secant method, or Newton's method. Spreadsheet =IRR() and the engine on this page use exactly such root-finding.

Algebraic IRR — simple special cases

CaseIRR formulaExample
Single lump sum at year nIRR = (Cₙ/|C₀|)^(1/n) − 1Outlay ₹5L, receive ₹8L in 5 yrs → (1.6)^0.2−1 = 9.86%
Level annuity, n yearsSolve PVIFA(IRR,n) = |C₀|/CFOutlay ₹5L, ₹1.6L/yr for 5 yrs → PVIFA=3.125 → IRR≈17%
Perpetual annuityIRR = CF/|C₀| − growth (if any)Outlay ₹10L, ₹1.4L/yr forever → 14%
Key insight: The IRR is intrinsic to the cash-flow pattern — it does not depend on your discount rate. You compute the IRR from the cash flows alone, then judge it against your WACC. This separation is why IRR feels "standalone," but also why it must always be interpreted relative to a hurdle rate.
Teaching note: Have students solve the single-lump-sum IRR by hand first (it only requires an nth root), then the annuity IRR by reading the PVIFA table backwards. Only then introduce the full polynomial and numerical methods — students appreciate why computers are needed.

The IRR decision rule

Standalone (independent) project: IRR > WACC → ACCEPT IRR = WACC → Indifferent IRR < WACC → REJECT Mutually exclusive projects: DO NOT simply pick the highest IRR — rank by NPV. (IRR can misrank; see Conflicts.) Capital rationing: IRR alone is insufficient; use Profitability Index (PI) to rank within the budget.

Common errors in IRR use

  • Picking the highest IRR among mutually exclusive projects
  • Forgetting IRR can have multiple or zero solutions
  • Treating IRR as a "safe" percentage without checking NPV
  • Ignoring the reinvestment-rate assumption
  • Comparing IRRs of projects with different lives

Calculation Methods for IRR

Because the IRR equation is generally a high-degree polynomial, we use practical solution techniques. All of them amount to finding the discount rate at which NPV = 0.

Method 1 — Trial-and-error with linear interpolation

1. Guess two rates r₁, r₂ that bracket zero: NPV(r₁) > 0 and NPV(r₂) < 0. 2. Linearly interpolate between them: IRR ≈ r₁ + (r₂ − r₁) × [ NPV(r₁) / (NPV(r₁) − NPV(r₂)) ] Worked example: Flows −₹1000, ₹300, ₹420, ₹680; r = 10% → NPV = +33.6; r = 15% → NPV = −58.9. IRR ≈ 10% + 5% × [33.6 / (33.6 − (−58.9))] = 10% + 5% × (33.6 / 92.5) = 10% + 5% × 0.363 = 11.8% (true IRR ≈ 12.0%)

Interpolation assumes the NPV curve is linear between r₁ and r₂ — a good approximation when the bracket is narrow, less so when it is wide.

Method 2 — Bisection (interval halving)

Start with a bracket [lo, hi] where NPV(lo) > 0 and NPV(hi) < 0. Repeat: mid = (lo + hi) / 2 if NPV(mid) > 0 → lo = mid else hi = mid until |NPV(mid)| < tolerance. Guaranteed to converge for any single root within the bracket — this is what the Live IRR Engine uses to find every IRR.

Method 3 — Newton–Raphson (fast, needs derivative)

r_{k+1} = r_k − NPV(r_k) / NPV'(r_k) where NPV'(r) = −Σ t·Cₜ/(1+r)^(t+1) Converges very fast (quadratically) when started near the root, but can diverge or jump to a different root if started poorly. Best for well-behaved, conventional cash flows.

Crossover rate (two projects)

When two NPV profiles intersect, the intersection rate is the crossover rate — where the projects have equal NPV. It equals the IRR of the incremental cash flows (Project B − Project A). Below it one project wins; above it the other does. The firm's actual WACC, not the average of the two IRRs, decides which is better. See the IRR vs NPV Compare tool.
Special Cases

Multiple IRRs — When One Answer Isn't Enough

A project can have more than one IRR when its cash flows change sign more than once. By Descartes' Rule of Signs, the number of positive IRRs is at most the number of sign changes in the cash-flow stream.

Classic example: Cash flows (₹): C₀ = −100, C₁ = +230, C₂ = −132 Sign changes: − → + (1st), + → − (2nd) ⇒ up to 2 positive IRRs. NPV(r) = −100 + 230/(1+r) − 132/(1+r)² Let x = 1/(1+r): −100 + 230x − 132x² = 0 ⇒ 132x² − 230x + 100 = 0 x = (230 ± √(52900 − 52800)) / 264 = (230 ± 10)/264 x = 0.9091 ⇒ r = 10.0% x = 0.8333 ⇒ r = 20.0% Two IRRs: 10% AND 20% — which is "the" IRR?

What this means for decision-making

  • With two IRRs, the simple rule "accept if IRR > WACC" breaks down. If WACC = 15%, the project's NPV is negative (between the two roots) even though 15% lies between two "IRR" values.
  • The accept/reject region is no longer a simple "left of IRR" zone — it can be a band.
  • Fix: Use NPV (which is unambiguous), or MIRR, or read the full profile to see all crossings.

Interactive — see both roots

Where multiple IRRs arise in practice: Projects with mid-life outflows — e.g., a nuclear plant (build, operate, then pay decommissioning costs), a mine (develop, extract, then reclaim), or any "borrow–repay–borrow" pattern. The Live IRR Engine finds all real roots in [−50%, 500%], so you will never be shown a single misleading IRR when several exist.
Teaching note: After showing the 10%/20% example, ask students to compute NPV at r = 15% and confirm it is negative. The punchline: "Both IRRs are 15%? No — at 15% the project loses money." This permanently fixes the idea that multiple IRRs invalidate the naive IRR rule.

Descartes' Rule of Signs (quick)

Count sign changes in the ordered cash-flow stream (C₀, C₁, …, Cₙ). · 1 sign change ⇒ at most 1 positive IRR (the normal case) · 2 sign changes ⇒ up to 2 positive IRRs · 3 sign changes ⇒ up to 3 positive IRRs Convention: Report all IRRs, then decide with NPV.

Special Cases

When IRR Is Undefined — No IRR or Negative IRR

IRR is not guaranteed to exist, and when it does it need not be positive.

Case 1 — No IRR (all cash flows same sign)

If every cash flow is positive (a pure inflow stream with no outlay) or every cash flow is negative, there is no discount rate that drives NPV to zero. IRR is simply undefined.

Example: Receive ₹100, ₹200, ₹300 with NO initial outflow. NPV(r) = 100/(1+r) + 200/(1+r)² + 300/(1+r)³ > 0 for all r ≥ 0. No root ⇒ IRR does not exist.

Case 2 — Negative IRR

A negative IRR occurs when even at a 0% discount rate the project destroys value — i.e., the sum of undiscounted inflows is less than the initial outlay. The break-even rate is below zero.

Example: C₀ = −1000, C₁ = 900. NPV(r) = −1000 + 900/(1+r) = 0 ⇒ (1+r) = 0.9 ⇒ r = −10%. The project returns less than even keeping the cash under the mattress.

Case 3 — IRR above 100% (or huge)

Very short, very front-loaded projects can show enormous IRRs. A ₹100 outlay returning ₹300 in one week has an IRR in the thousands of percent — technically correct but economically meaningless without context (and it ignores reinvestment).

SituationIRR behaviourWhat to do
All CFs same signNo real IRRUse NPV; IRR unavailable
Undiscounted total < outlayNegative IRRReject; NPV < 0 at r=0
Very short, front-loadedIRR > 100%Check NPV; watch reinvestment
Conventional (− then +)One positive IRRStandard IRR rule applies
Takeaway: IRR is a derived quantity that can misbehave. NPV is the primitive, always-well-defined metric. Whenever IRR looks strange, fall back to NPV.
Teaching note: The "negative IRR" case is a great reality check — students expect IRR to always be a nice positive percentage. Showing that a project can have IRR = −10% (worse than doing nothing) cements the link between IRR and the time value of money.

Quick NPV reality check

Before trusting an IRR, compute NPV at r = 0%: NPV(0) = (sum of all inflows) − (initial outlay) · NPV(0) < 0 ⇒ IRR is negative or undefined · NPV(0) > 0 ⇒ at least one positive IRR exists (for conventional CFs) This single calculation predicts whether a sensible IRR even exists.
Special Cases

The Reinvestment Assumption — IRR's Hidden Flaw

The most important theoretical criticism of IRR: it implicitly assumes that all intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself. NPV, by contrast, assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital (WACC) — the rate the firm can actually earn.

Why this matters

IRR assumption: Each ₹1 received early is reinvested to earn the project's own IRR. If IRR = 40% but the firm can only reinvest at 12% (WACC), the IRR overstates the project's true compounded return. NPV assumption: Intermediate CFs are reinvested at WACC — the realistic, market-based rate. A high-IRR project is therefore "penalised" by reality: its early cash flows cannot actually be reinvested at that lofty rate, so its real wealth contribution is lower than its IRR suggests.

Worked illustration

Two projects, each ICF = ₹1,000, each returns a total of ₹1,500, but timed differently. WACC = 12%.

ProjectCFsIRRNPV @12%True compounded value @12%
A (early)₹1,500 at Yr 150.0%+₹339₹1,500×1.12 = ₹1,680
B (late)₹1,500 at Yr 58.4%−₹149₹1,500 (no reinvest)

IRR ranks A far above B (50% vs 8.4%), but that ranking presumes A's early ₹1,500 can be reinvested at 50%. At the realistic 12%, A compounds to only ₹1,680 — and NPV already reflects that. NPV is the consistent, assumption-honest metric.

The fix — MIRR: Modified IRR forces an explicit, realistic reinvestment rate (the firm's cost of capital or a specified rate), eliminating the hidden assumption. See the MIRR page. MIRR always yields a single, economically sensible rate.
Teaching note: The reinvestment critique is the deepest reason IRR can conflict with NPV. Show students the "compounded value at WACC" column and let them see that IRR's number implicitly requires reinvesting at IRR. Once they grasp this, MIRR feels like a natural cure rather than a separate topic.

IRR vs MIRR — assumption

IRR: reinvest at IRR (often unrealistically high).

MIRR: reinvest at a stated reinvestment rate (usually WACC) and finance outflows at a finance rate.

MIRR ≤ IRR when the project's IRR exceeds the reinvestment rate (the usual case). MIRR gives one answer and no multiple-root problem.
Other Metrics

Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR)

MIRR fixes IRR's two weaknesses — the multiple-root problem and the unrealistic reinvestment assumption — by using two explicit rates: a finance rate (to discount outflows) and a reinvestment rate (to compound inflows).

The MIRR formula

Step 1 — Terminal Value (TV): Compound all positive CFs to the end (year n) at the reinvestment rate rr: TV = Σ_{Cₜ > 0} Cₜ × (1 + rr)^(n − t) Step 2 — Present Value of Costs (PVC): Discount all negative CFs to time 0 at the finance rate f: PVC = Σ_{Cₜ < 0} |Cₜ| / (1 + f)^t Step 3 — MIRR: MIRR = ( TV / PVC )^(1/n) − 1 MIRR is a SINGLE, well-defined rate — no multiple answers, no hidden reinvestment at IRR.

Worked example

Flows (₹): C₀ = −1000, C₁ = 300, C₂ = 420, C₃ = 680. f = 12%, rr = 12%, n = 3. TV = 300×(1.12)² + 420×1.12 + 680 = 376.3 + 470.4 + 680 = 1,526.7 PVC = 1000 (only the initial outlay is negative) MIRR = (1,526.7 / 1,000)^(1/3) − 1 = (1.5267)^0.333 − 1 = 15.2% (Compare IRR ≈ 12.0% — MIRR is higher here because rr > IRR is not the case; the difference reflects the explicit terminal-value treatment.)

When MIRR ≠ IRR

ConditionRelationReason
IRR > reinvest rate (typical)MIRR < IRRIRR overstated the reinvestment opportunity

Recommendation: When communicating a percentage return for a project with non-conventional cash flows or a very high IRR, report MIRR alongside (or instead of) IRR. It is the "honest IRR."
Teaching note: Have students compute both IRR and MIRR for the multiple-IRR example (−100, +230, −132). The original has two IRRs (10%, 20%); MIRR collapses them to one sensible number — a powerful demonstration of MIRR's value.

Other Metrics

XIRR — Internal Rate of Return for Dated Cash Flows

IRR assumes cash flows occur at exact, evenly-spaced periods (year-end). Real investments — private equity, venture capital, irregular dividends, machinery bought mid-year — don't. XIRR extends IRR to cash flows that occur on specific calendar dates, discounting by the exact elapsed time.

The XIRR equation

0 = Σ Cᵢ / (1 + XIRR)^( (dᵢ − d₀) / 365 ) where dᵢ is the date of cash flow i, d₀ is the date of the first cash flow, and the exponent is the fraction of a year elapsed. This is identical in spirit to IRR — find the rate that sets present value to zero — but with precise day-counting instead of whole periods.

Why XIRR matters

  • Accuracy: A flow on 5 April vs 31 December in the same "year" is materially different; XIRR captures it.
  • Industry standard: XIRR is the metric PE/VC funds report because capital calls and distributions are highly irregular.
  • Handles any timing: Negative and positive flows can interleave on any dates; XIRR still solves for the rate.

Interactive XIRR

Excel/Sheets: =XIRR(values, dates, [guess]). The function uses Newton's method on the dated-NPV equation. If cash flows never change sign, XIRR returns #NUM! (no solution) — same "no IRR" issue as regular IRR.
Teaching note: Give students a simple two-date example: invest ₹10,00,000 on 1 Jan 2024, receive ₹12,00,000 on 1 Jul 2024 (181 days). XIRR ≈ (1,200,000/1,000,000)^(365/181) − 1 ≈ 44.3% — far higher than the naive 20% (which would ignore that the money was returned in half a year.

XIRR vs IRR — key difference

IRR: whole-period discounting, Cₜ at t = 1,2,3…

XIRR: date-exact discounting, Cᵢ at actual days elapsed.

Use IRR for textbook annual CFs; use XIRR for anything with real calendar dates. For annual-end CFs, XIRR ≈ IRR.
Comparison — Side by Side

IRR vs NPV — Placed Side by Side

The two metrics answer different questions. IRR asks "what is the break-even rate of return?" (a percentage, good for communication). NPV asks "how many rupees of wealth does this create?" (an absolute amount, the correct decision metric). Here they sit directly next to each other.

Metric 1

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

  • Unit: a percentage return (%).
  • Question: At what discount rate does NPV = 0?
  • Strength: intuitive, scale-free, easy to compare to a hurdle rate.
  • Can give multiple or no answers.
  • Assumes reinvestment at the IRR.
  • Can misrank mutually exclusive projects (scale, timing).
  • Not additive — you cannot average IRRs.
Best used for: communicating a project's return, screening, and cross-checking NPV.
Metric 2

Net Present Value (NPV)

  • Unit: rupees of wealth (₹).
  • Question: How much value does the project add at the WACC?
  • Strength: always a unique, well-defined answer.
  • Correct for mutually exclusive projects (pick highest NPV).
  • Realistic reinvestment at WACC.
  • Additive: NPV(A+B) = NPV(A) + NPV(B).
  • Directly linked to shareholder wealth.
Best used for: the primary accept/reject and ranking decision — always.

Comparison

IRR vs NPV — The Classic Conflicts

For independent, conventional projects, IRR and NPV always agree. They disagree for mutually exclusive projects, and the disagreement has three well-known causes.

1 · Scale problem

Project A (small): ICF ₹1,000; CF ₹1,500 at Yr 1.
IRR = 50%; NPV@10% = +₹364.
High return, small absolute value.
Project B (large): ICF ₹10,000; CF ₹12,000 at Yr 1.
IRR = 20%; NPV@10% = +₹909.
Lower return, larger absolute value.

IRR says A (50% > 20%); NPV says B (₹909 > ₹364). Since the projects are mutually exclusive, the firm should pick B — it creates more wealth. IRR misleads because it ignores scale.

2 · Timing problem

Project S (front-loaded): ICF ₹1,000; CF ₹1,100 at Yr 1.
IRR = 10%; NPV@5% = +₹48.
Project L (back-loaded): ICF ₹1,000; CF ₹2,000 at Yr 10.
IRR = 7.2%; NPV@5% = +₹228.

IRR says S (10% > 7.2%); NPV says L (₹228 > ₹48) at WACC = 5%. IRR favours the early-returning project even though L creates more total wealth. The ranking depends on the WACC relative to the crossover rate.

3 · Mutually exclusive with different lives

Comparing a 3-year machine to a 10-year machine using raw IRR/NPV is invalid — you must use equivalent annual annuity (EAA) or a common reinvestment horizon. IRR is especially dangerous here because it says nothing about duration.

The resolution — incremental analysis

To choose between mutually exclusive A and B (with B larger): 1. Compute the INCREMENTAL cash flows: Δ = B − A. 2. Compute IRR(Δ) — the crossover rate. 3. If IRR(Δ) > WACC → choose B (the larger project). If IRR(Δ) < WACC → choose A. This makes IRR consistent with NPV — because you are now evaluating one "project" (the increment) the same way you would standalone.
Rule of thumb: For mutually exclusive projects, rank by NPV (or by the IRR of the incremental cash flows). Never rank by standalone IRR.
Teaching note: Use the "₹1,000 vs ₹10,000" scale example on day one — it is the single most effective demonstration that IRR can be wrong. Students remember that a 50% return on ₹1,000 is still only ₹500, while a 20% return on ₹10,000 is ₹2,000.

Crossover rate

The crossover rate is the discount rate where two projects have equal NPV. It is the IRR of the incremental (B − A) cash flows. Below crossover: the steeper/larger profile has higher NPV. Above crossover: the other has higher NPV. The firm's actual WACC decides — not the average of the two IRRs. Try it in the Compare tool.

Comprehensive Worked Examples

Example 1 — Basic IRR (interpolation)

Flows: −₹1,000; ₹300; ₹420; ₹680. Find IRR by trial and check against NPV at 12%.

rNPV(₹)
10%+33.6
15%−58.9

Interpolate: IRR ≈ 10% + 5% × 33.6/(33.6+58.9) = 11.8% (true ≈ 12.0%).
Since IRR > WACC 12%? It is ≈12% — borderline accept. Decision hinges on precise WACC.

Example 2 — Annuity IRR

ICF = ₹5,00,000; ₹1,60,000/yr for 5 yrs. IRR?

PVIFA(IRR,5) = 5,00,000/1,60,000 = 3.125.

From PVIFA table: at 17%, PVIFA=3.199; at 18%, PVIFA=3.127; at 19%, PVIFA=3.058. → IRR ≈ 18%.

At WACC 12%: NPV = 1,60,000×3.6048 − 5,00,000 = +₹76,768 → ACCEPT.

Example 3 — Multiple IRRs

Flows: −₹100; +₹230; −₹132. Show the two IRRs.

Solve −100 + 230/(1+r) − 132/(1+r)² = 0. Let x=1/(1+r): 132x² − 230x + 100 = 0 → x = 0.9091 or 0.8333.

IRR = 10% and IRR = 20%. At WACC 15%, NPV = −100 + 230/1.15 − 132/1.3225 = −100 + 200 − 99.8 = −₹0.2 (≈0, slightly negative). IRR rule is ambiguous — use NPV.

Example 4 — MIRR

Flows: −₹1,000; ₹300; ₹420; ₹680. f = 12%, rr = 12%, n = 3. MIRR?

TV = 300×1.12² + 420×1.12 + 680 = 376.3 + 470.4 + 680 = 1,526.7.
PVC = 1,000.

MIRR = (1,526.7/1,000)^(1/3) − 1 = 15.2%.

Example 5 — XIRR

Invest ₹10,00,000 on 1 Jan 2024; receive ₹12,00,000 on 1 Jul 2024 (181 days). XIRR?

XIRR = (1,200,000/1,000,000)^(365/181) − 1 = 1.2^2.0177 − 1 = ≈ 44.3%.

The money was returned in half a year, so the annualised return is far above the naive 20%.

Example 6 — Scale conflict

A: ICF ₹1,000, ₹1,500 at Yr1. B: ICF ₹10,000, ₹12,000 at Yr1. WACC 10%. Which to accept if mutually exclusive?

A: IRR 50%, NPV = 1,500/1.1 − 1,000 = +₹364.
B: IRR 20%, NPV = 12,000/1.1 − 10,000 = +₹909.

IRR prefers A; NPV prefers B. Since mutually exclusive, accept B (more wealth). Incremental (B−A): ICF ₹9,000, CF ₹10,500 at Yr1 → IRR = 16.7% > 10% WACC → choose B. Consistent.

Example 7 — Reinvestment illustration

A: ₹1,500 at Yr1; B: ₹1,500 at Yr5; both ICF ₹1,000; WACC 12%. Compare IRR vs NPV.

A: IRR 50%, NPV@12% = 1,500/1.12 − 1,000 = +₹339.
B: IRR 8.4%, NPV@12% = 1,500/1.7623 − 1,000 = −₹149.

IRR ranks A ≫ B; NPV agrees A > B here, but the 50% IRR implicitly assumes reinvesting ₹1,500 at 50% — unrealistic. At the real 12%, A compounds to only ₹1,680.

Example 8 — No IRR

Flows: +₹100; +₹200; +₹300 (no initial outlay). IRR?

NPV(r) = 100/(1+r) + 200/(1+r)² + 300/(1+r)³ > 0 for all r ≥ 0. There is no discount rate that makes NPV zero → IRR does not exist. IRR is undefined; the project is a pure inflow with no investment benchmark.

IRR Formula Bank

Core IRR

0 = C₀ + Σ Cₜ/(1+IRR)^t (t=1..n) IRR = rate where NPV = 0 Accept: IRR > WACC | Reject: IRR < WACC

Interpolation

IRR ≈ r₁ + (r₂−r₁) × NPV(r₁) / [NPV(r₁) − NPV(r₂)] with NPV(r₁)>0, NPV(r₂)<0

Simple closed forms

Single CF at n: IRR=(Cₙ/|C₀|)^(1/n)−1 Annuity: solve PVIFA(IRR,n)=|C₀|/CF Perpetuity: IRR = CF/|C₀|

MIRR

TV = Σ_{Cₜ>0} Cₜ(1+rr)^(n−t) PVC = Σ_{Cₜ<0} |Cₜ|/(1+f)^t MIRR = (TV/PVC)^(1/n) − 1

XIRR

0 = Σ Cᵢ/(1+XIRR)^((dᵢ−d₀)/365) dᵢ = date of flow i; d₀ = first date

Crossover (incremental) IRR

IRR( B − A ) = crossover rate If > WACC → choose larger project B If < WACC → choose project A

NPV (for comparison)

NPV = Σ Cₜ/(1+WACC)^t − C₀ Rank mutually exclusive by NPV
