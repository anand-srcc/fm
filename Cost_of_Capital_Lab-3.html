Foundations

Cost of Capital — Concept

The cost of capital is the minimum rate of return that a firm must earn on its investments to satisfy the expectations of its investors — debt holders, preference shareholders, and equity shareholders — given the risk they bear.

It is simultaneously:

  • A cut-off rate / hurdle rate for investment decisions — accept projects only if their expected return ≥ cost of capital.
  • The discount rate for NPV computation — projects are worth undertaking only if their NPV is positive when discounted at the cost of capital.
  • The benchmark for capital structure decisions — a firm should choose the financing mix that minimises its overall (weighted average) cost of capital.
  • The required rate of return for investors — the opportunity cost of investing in the firm versus alternatives of equal risk.
Key insight: The cost of capital is not simply the interest rate on debt. It is the blended, risk-adjusted return required by all providers of capital — debt is the cheapest (tax-deductible, low risk), preference shares are intermediate, and equity is the most expensive (highest risk, residual claimant).
Teaching note: Students often confuse "cost of capital" with "interest rate on a loan." Emphasise that cost of capital encompasses ALL sources of finance, is after-tax for debt, and represents an opportunity cost — what investors could earn elsewhere at equivalent risk.

Sources of capital

Debt
Loans, debentures, bonds. Interest is tax-deductible → cheapest source after-tax.
Preference Shares
Fixed dividend, priority over equity. Dividend NOT tax-deductible → costlier than debt.
Equity (Ordinary Shares)
Residual claimants, highest risk → highest required return. Includes retained earnings.
WACC
Weighted blend of all three — the overall cost of capital.
Foundations

Significance of Cost of Capital

1. Investment (Capital Budgeting) Decisions
The cost of capital is the discount rate used in NPV analysis. It is the hurdle rate — projects returning more than the cost of capital create value; those returning less destroy it.
2. Capital Structure Decisions (Financing Decisions)
Firms choose the mix of debt, preference, and equity to minimise WACC. The optimal capital structure is the one that minimises WACC (Traditional Theory view), thereby maximising firm value.
3. Evaluation of Financial Performance
Actual returns are compared against the cost of capital. Earning above WACC = value creation (positive EVA); earning below WACC = value destruction.
4. Dividend Policy Decisions
Whether to retain profits or pay dividends depends on whether the firm can earn more than the cost of equity on retained earnings (Gordon's growth model argument).
5. Leasing vs Buying Decisions
The cost of capital is the appropriate discount rate for comparing the NPV of owning versus leasing an asset.
6. Regulation of Public Utilities
Regulatory bodies (like SEBI, TRAI) use cost of capital to set fair rates of return for regulated utilities, ensuring investors earn a competitive return without excessive monopoly profits.
Component Costs

Cost of Debt (Kd)

The cost of debt is the effective rate the firm pays on its borrowed funds. Since interest payments are tax-deductible, the after-tax cost of debt is lower than the pre-tax rate — creating the famous "tax shield on debt."

1. Irredeemable (Perpetual) Debt

Before-tax Kd = I / NP After-tax Kd = I(1−Tc) / NP where: I = Annual interest payment (₹) NP = Net proceeds from debt issue (Market Price − Flotation Costs) Tc = Corporate tax rate

2. Redeemable Debt (most common)

Approximate Kd (after-tax): Kd = [I(1−Tc) + (RV − NP)/n] / [(RV + NP)/2] where: I = Annual interest = Coupon Rate × Face Value Tc = Tax rate RV = Redemption Value (Face Value if at par) NP = Net proceeds (issue price − flotation costs) n = Years to maturity / redemption
Interpretation: The numerator is the annual after-tax interest PLUS the annual amortisation of the discount (or MINUS the premium) on the debt. The denominator is the average of the issue price and redemption value — the average amount of money the firm has outstanding.

3. Exact Kd — IRR / YTM Method

Find Kd such that: NP = Σ [I(1−Tc)/(1+Kd)ᵗ] + RV/(1+Kd)ⁿ This is the after-tax Yield to Maturity (YTM) — solved by trial and error / linear interpolation. Interpolation formula: Kd = L + [NPV_L / (NPV_L − NPV_H)] × (H − L) where L and H are the trial rates.

Key points

AspectTreatment
Interest is tax-deductible?Yes — use after-tax Kd

Exam tip: Always use after-tax Kd in WACC — interest tax savings reduce the effective cost to the firm. This is one of the most tested distinctions in cost of capital problems.

Illustration

10% debentures, Face Value ₹1,000, issued at ₹950 (flotation ₹20), redeemable at par after 5 years, tax rate 30%.

NP = 950 − 20 = ₹930; RV = ₹1,000; n = 5 I = 10% × 1,000 = ₹100 I(1−Tc) = 100(0.70) = ₹70 Kd = [70 + (1000−930)/5] / [(1000+930)/2] = [70 + 14] / [965] = 84 / 965 = 8.70%
Component Costs

Cost of Preference Shares (Kp)

Preference dividend is paid from after-tax profits — it is NOT tax-deductible. Therefore, unlike debt, there is no tax adjustment for preference shares.

1. Irredeemable Preference Shares

Kp = PD / NP where: PD = Annual preference dividend (₹) = Dividend Rate × Face Value NP = Net proceeds = Issue Price − Flotation Costs

2. Redeemable Preference Shares

Approximate Kp: Kp = [PD + (RV − NP)/n] / [(RV + NP)/2] where: PD = Annual preference dividend RV = Redemption Value NP = Net proceeds n = Years to redemption
Why Kp > Kd (after-tax)? Interest on debt is tax-deductible, giving debt a tax shield. Preference dividend is not deductible — the firm pays it from after-tax profits. So despite preference shares being safer than equity (priority in dividends and liquidation), Kp exceeds the after-tax Kd.

Illustration

12% preference shares, Face Value ₹100, issued at ₹95 (flotation ₹3), redeemable at par after 6 years.

NP = 95 − 3 = ₹92; PD = 12% × 100 = ₹12; RV = ₹100 Kp = [12 + (100−92)/6] / [(100+92)/2] = [12 + 1.33] / [96] = 13.33 / 96 = 13.89%

Component Costs

Cost of Equity — Dividend Discount Model (DDM / Gordon Model)

The cost of equity (Ke) is the return shareholders require to compensate for the risk of holding the firm's shares. Under the DDM, the current market price equals the present value of all future dividends, which gives us Ke.

Case 1 — Zero Growth (No Growth)

P₀ = D / Ke → Ke = D / P₀ = D / NP (new issue) where: D = Annual dividend per share (constant forever) P₀ = Current market price per share NP = Net proceeds per share (new issue, after flotation)

Case 2 — Constant Growth (Gordon Model — most common)

P₀ = D₁ / (Ke − g) → Ke = D₁/P₀ + g (existing shares, no flotation cost) → Ke = D₁/NP + g (new issue, with flotation cost) where: D₁ = Expected dividend next year = D₀ × (1 + g) D₀ = Current / most recent dividend g = Constant annual dividend growth rate P₀ = Current market price NP = Net proceeds = P₀ − Flotation costs

Estimating g — the growth rate

Method 1 — Retention / Internal Growth Rate: g = b × ROE where b = retention ratio = 1 − Dividend Payout Ratio ROE = Return on Equity Method 2 — Historical trend in dividends: g = (Dₙ/D₀)^(1/n) − 1 (CAGR of dividends over n years)

Illustration

Share price ₹80. Current dividend ₹5. Growth rate 6%. New issue flotation ₹8.

D₁ = 5 × 1.06 = ₹5.30 Ke (existing shares): Ke = 5.30/80 + 0.06 = 6.625% + 6% = 12.625% Ke (new issue): NP = 80 − 8 = ₹72 Ke = 5.30/72 + 0.06 = 7.36% + 6% = 13.36%
Key observation: Ke for new equity is always higher than for existing equity due to flotation costs reducing the net proceeds. This is why internally generated equity (retained earnings) is preferred over new equity issues — no flotation cost.

Limitations of DDM

1. Requires constant dividend growth assumption — unrealistic for many firms.
2. Cannot be used for zero-dividend firms (growth companies, startups).
3. Sensitive to the assumed growth rate — small changes in g dramatically change Ke.
Component Costs

Cost of Equity — Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

CAPM derives the required return on equity by quantifying the systematic (market) risk of the stock, measured by Beta (β). It is the most theoretically rigorous approach to estimating Ke.

CAPM: Ke = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf) where: Ke = Required return on equity (cost of equity) Rf = Risk-free rate (yield on government securities / T-bills) β (Beta) = Systematic risk of the stock relative to the market β > 1 → more volatile than market β = 1 → moves exactly with market β < 1 → less volatile than market β = 0 → risk-free asset Rm = Expected return on the market portfolio (Rm−Rf) = Market Risk Premium (MRP) — extra return for taking on market risk

Security Market Line (SML)

The SML is the graphical representation of CAPM — a straight line plotting Ke against β. Every asset correctly priced by CAPM lies exactly on the SML. Assets above the SML are underpriced (earn more than required) and below are overpriced.

SML: Ke = Rf + β × MRP At β=0: Ke = Rf (risk-free return) At β=1: Ke = Rm (market return) For each unit increase in β: Ke rises by MRP

Illustration

Rf = 7%, Rm = 13%, β = 1.4.

MRP = 13% − 7% = 6% Ke = 7% + 1.4 × 6% = 7% + 8.4% = 15.4%

SML: Ke vs Beta. The point marked ★ corresponds to the illustration above (β=1.4, Ke=15.4%).

Component Costs

Cost of Retained Earnings (Kr)

Retained earnings are profits not distributed as dividends. They are NOT free — they represent an opportunity cost to equity shareholders who could have received dividends and invested elsewhere at equivalent risk.

Three approaches to estimating Kr

Method 1 — Opportunity Cost Approach: Kr = Ke (cost of existing equity) Rationale: Shareholders could earn Ke if they received dividends and invested in similar-risk shares. Retaining earnings forces them to forego this return. Method 2 — CAPM Approach: Kr = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf) Same as Ke via CAPM — no flotation cost adjustment needed. Method 3 — External Yield Criterion / Bond-Yield Plus: Kr = Yield on government bonds + Risk premium (a heuristic — less theoretically rigorous)
Key distinction: Kr vs Ke (new issue)
Kr = Ke − (flotation cost adjustment)
Retained earnings avoid flotation costs entirely — no new shares are issued. Therefore Kr is always ≤ Ke for new equity. In practice, Kr is often taken as equal to Ke (existing shares) in WACC if no flotation cost is involved in the equity figure used.

Why retained earnings are not free

Opportunity cost argument: Shareholders could have invested the retained funds in alternative investments of equal risk and earned a return of Ke. By retaining, the firm implicitly asks shareholders to "accept" this return — making Kr = Ke the opportunity cost.

WACC

Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

WACC is the overall cost of capital to the firm — the weighted average of the after-tax costs of all its financing sources, where the weights reflect each source's proportion in the total capital structure.

WACC = Wd × Kd(1−Tc) + Wp × Kp + We × Ke where: Wd = Weight of debt = D / (D+P+E) Wp = Weight of preference = P / (D+P+E) We = Weight of equity = E / (D+P+E) Kd(1−Tc) = After-tax cost of debt Kp = Cost of preference (no tax adjustment) Ke = Cost of equity (DDM or CAPM) Weights sum to 1: Wd + Wp + We = 1

Two weighting systems

SystemWeights based onValues used
Book Value (BV) WeightsBalance sheet valuesHistorical cost — may differ significantly from market
Market Value (MV) WeightsCurrent market pricesReflects true market proportions — theoretically preferred
Which weights to use? Market value weights are theoretically superior — they reflect current market perceptions and the actual cost of raising capital today. Book value weights are simpler (data readily available from balance sheet) and are more stable over time. Both methods are tested in examinations.

Why WACC matters

Investment decisions: WACC is the hurdle rate — projects must return at least WACC to create value.
Firm valuation: The firm's total value = EBIT(1−Tc) / WACC (NOI model) — lower WACC = higher firm value.
Capital structure optimisation: The optimal capital structure minimises WACC.
Teaching note: WACC is the most important single number in corporate finance. It connects investment decisions (NPV), financing decisions (capital structure), and valuation. Ensure students understand that WACC is a marginal concept — it should use marginal (current) costs, not historical costs.

Advantages of BV weights

Easy to compute — data directly from balance sheet.
Stable over time — not affected by market price fluctuations.
Useful when market prices are not available (private firms).

Disadvantages of BV weights

Historical cost may differ greatly from current market value.
Does not reflect the current cost of raising new capital.
Can be misleading when the firm's market value differs significantly from book value (e.g., high-growth firms with large intangibles).

Market value weights use the current market prices of debt, preference, and equity — reflecting what it would actually cost the firm to raise capital today.

Theoretically superior — reflects current market conditions and the true cost of capital.

Used in practice by investment banks and sophisticated firms for project evaluation.

Disadvantages of MV weights

Market prices fluctuate — WACC changes daily.
Not available for unlisted (private) firms.
Higher administrative burden to compute regularly.

Why MV WACC usually differs from BV WACC

In most listed firms, equity market value > book value (Tobin's Q > 1). This means:

Market weights give more weight to equity (the most expensive source) than book weights do. Therefore, MV WACC > BV WACC for most healthy, growing firms.
Advanced

Marginal Cost of Capital (MCC)

The Marginal Cost of Capital (MCC) is the cost of raising one additional rupee of capital — the weighted average cost of the next unit of financing. Unlike the historical WACC (based on existing capital), MCC is forward-looking and determines the cut-off for new investment decisions.

Why MCC rises as more capital is raised

As a firm raises more and more capital in a given period, it exhausts cheaper sources first. At some point — the Break-Even Point (BEP) — a component's cost jumps (e.g., retained earnings are exhausted and the firm must issue new equity at a higher cost). This raises the MCC.

Break-Even Point (BEP) for retained earnings: BEP = Retained Earnings available / Weight of equity in target structure Example: Retained earnings = ₹6,00,000; Equity weight = 60%: BEP = 6,00,000 / 0.60 = ₹10,00,000 Interpretation: When total capital raised exceeds ₹10,00,000, the firm must use more expensive new equity (Ke > Kr), causing the MCC to jump upward at this point.

MCC Schedule

A table showing MCC at different levels of total new capital raised — starting lower (when cheaper retained earnings are available) and stepping up at each break-even point.

MCC Schedule: flat at 11% up to ₹10L (within retained earnings), then steps up to 12.2% as new (costlier) equity must be issued.

Decision rule: Accept investment projects whose expected return (IRR) exceeds the MCC at the level of investment required. The optimal investment budget is where the Investment Opportunity Schedule (IOS) intersects the MCC curve.
Advanced

Investment Opportunity Schedule (IOS) & Optimal Budget

The Investment Opportunity Schedule (IOS) is a ranking of all available positive-NPV investment opportunities, plotted from highest to lowest expected return (IRR), against the cumulative capital required. It represents the firm's demand for capital.

The optimal capital budget is determined by the intersection of the IOS and the MCC curve — at this point, the marginal return from new investment exactly equals the marginal cost of new capital.

Accept all projects whose IRR (IOS) lies above the MCC line. The optimal budget is at the intersection (here ~₹12L). Projects beyond this point earn less than they cost to finance.

Flotation Costs & Their Effect on Cost of Capital

Flotation costs (also called issue costs or underwriting costs) are the expenses incurred in issuing new securities — brokerage, underwriting fees, registration fees, printing, etc. They reduce the net proceeds received by the firm from the issue, thereby raising the effective cost of capital.

Effect on each component

SourceWith Flotation Cost (f)Formula
DebtNP = Issue Price × (1−f)Kd = I(1−Tc)/NP
PreferenceNP = Issue Price × (1−f)Kp = PD/NP (irred.)
Equity (DDM)NP = P₀ × (1−f)Ke = D₁/NP + g
Retained EarningsNo issue → no flotation costKr = D₁/P₀ + g
Key conclusion: Flotation costs raise the cost of all externally issued securities. Retained earnings, which require no new issue, are therefore always cheaper than new equity — making internal financing (retained earnings) preferable to external equity on cost grounds, consistent with the Pecking Order Theory.

Two methods to handle flotation costs in NPV

Method 1 — Adjust the discount rate (as above): Use post-flotation Ke/Kd in WACC → higher WACC Method 2 — Adjust the initial investment: Add flotation costs to the initial outlay (C₀) Use the unadjusted WACC as the discount rate Method 2 is theoretically preferred for project evaluation because flotation costs are a one-time cash outflow, not a perpetual increase in cost.

Illustration

Current share price ₹100. Flotation cost 5%. D₁ = ₹8. g = 7%.

NP = 100 × (1−0.05) = ₹95 Ke (without flotation) = 8/100 + 0.07 = 15% Ke (with flotation) = 8/95 + 0.07 = 8.42% + 7% = 15.42% The 5% flotation cost raises Ke by 0.42 percentage points — a meaningful difference at scale.
Exam tip: When a question states "flotation costs" or "issue costs" for shares, use NP = Market Price − Flotation Costs (or Market Price × (1−f%) depending on how it is given) in the denominator of the Ke formula. For retained earnings, always use Market Price (P₀) — no adjustment.

Example 1 — Cost of Redeemable Debt

12% debentures, FV ₹1,000, issued at ₹980, flotation ₹15, redeemable at ₹1,050 after 6 years. Tax rate 35%. Compute Kd (after-tax) using the approximate method.

NP = 980 − 15 = ₹965; I = 12% × 1,000 = ₹120; I(1−Tc) = 120 × 0.65 = ₹78; RV = ₹1,050; n = 6

Capital gain = (1,050 − 965)/6 = 85/6 = ₹14.17

Avg funds = (1,050 + 965)/2 = ₹1,007.50

Kd = (78 + 14.17) / 1,007.50 = 92.17 / 1,007.50 = 9.15%

Example 2 — Cost of Redeemable Preference

10% preference shares, FV ₹100, issued at ₹95, flotation 3%, redeemable at par after 8 years. Compute Kp.

Issue price = ₹95; Flotation = 3% × 95 = ₹2.85; NP = 95 − 2.85 = ₹92.15

PD = 10% × 100 = ₹10; RV = ₹100; n = 8

Capital gain = (100 − 92.15)/8 = 7.85/8 = ₹0.98

Avg funds = (100 + 92.15)/2 = ₹96.08

Kp = (10 + 0.98) / 96.08 = 10.98 / 96.08 = 11.43%

Example 3 — Ke by Gordon Model & CAPM

Share price ₹120. Last dividend ₹6. Growth 8%. Beta 1.3, Rf 7%, Rm 14%. Flotation on new issue: ₹10 per share. Compute Ke (existing), Ke (new issue), Kr, and CAPM-based Ke.

D₁ = 6 × 1.08 = ₹6.48

Ke (existing / Kr) = 6.48/120 + 0.08 = 5.40% + 8% = 13.40%

Ke (new issue): NP = 120 − 10 = ₹110 → Ke = 6.48/110 + 0.08 = 5.89% + 8% = 13.89%

CAPM: Ke = 7% + 1.3 × (14%−7%) = 7% + 9.1% = 16.10%

Note: DDM (13.4%) and CAPM (16.1%) give different results — use whichever is specified, or average them.

Example 6 — Marginal Cost & Break-Even Point

Target capital structure: Debt 40%, Equity 60%. Kd(after-tax) = 7%. Kr (retained earnings) = 14%. Ke (new equity) = 16%. Retained earnings available = ₹4,80,000. Find the BEP and MCC schedule.

BEP = Retained Earnings / Equity Weight = 4,80,000 / 0.60 = ₹8,00,000

WACC₁ (up to ₹8L): 0.40×7% + 0.60×14% = 2.8% + 8.4% = 11.20%

WACC₂ (beyond ₹8L): 0.40×7% + 0.60×16% = 2.8% + 9.6% = 12.40%

Accept all projects with IRR ≥ 11.20% up to a total capital budget of ₹8L. For additional investment beyond ₹8L, the hurdle rate rises to 12.40%. Only accept projects returning ≥ 12.40% from that point.

Kr = D₁/P₀ + g (no flotation cost adjustment) = Ke for existing shares

WACC

WACC = Wd×Kd(1−Tc) + Wp×Kp + We×Ke Wd + Wp + We = 1 Use BV or MV weights as specified

MCC Break-Even Point

BEP = Retained Earnings / Weight of Equity Above BEP: use Ke (new issue) instead of Kr → MCC rises
