Foundations

Cost of Equity — Introduction

The cost of equity (Ke) is the minimum rate of return that equity shareholders require to compensate them for the risk of investing in the firm's shares. It is the opportunity cost of equity capital — what shareholders could earn by investing in alternative investments of equivalent risk.

Why cost of equity is difficult to measure

Unlike debt (where the interest rate is contractually specified) or preference shares (where the dividend rate is fixed), equity carries no contractual obligation. The dividend is at the discretion of the Board. There is no stated rate. Ke must therefore be inferred from observable market data using financial models.

Three main approaches

1. Dividend Discount Model (DDM) / Gordon Model
Infers Ke from the current market price, current/expected dividend, and the expected growth rate. Based on the principle that price = PV of all future dividends.
2. Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)
Estimates Ke using the stock's systematic risk (Beta), the risk-free rate, and the market risk premium. Theoretically most rigorous — accounts for risk explicitly.
3. Bond Yield Plus Risk Premium
Adds a subjective risk premium to the firm's own cost of debt (YTM on bonds). Simple heuristic — less precise but quick.

Foundations

Why Ke is the Highest Cost of Capital

In a firm's capital structure, the cost of capital follows a clear hierarchy: Kd (after-tax) < Kp < Kr < Ke (new issue). Understanding why is fundamental to capital structure theory.

SourceRisk to InvestorTax BenefitResult
Debt (Kd)Lowest — secured, priority in liquidation, fixed returnYes — interest tax-deductibleCheapest
Preference (Kp)Medium — priority over equity, fixed dividendNo — dividend not deductibleMiddle
Retained Earnings (Kr)High — residual claim, no guaranteeNo tax benefitHigh
New Equity (Ke)Highest — residual, plus flotation cost burdenNo tax benefit + flotation costHighest
The two reasons Ke exceeds Kr:
1. No tax shield: Dividends are paid from after-tax profits — unlike interest, they are not deductible. The government does not subsidise equity finance.
2. Flotation costs (for new issue): Issuing new shares incurs brokerage, underwriting, and legal costs that reduce net proceeds below market price, raising the effective cost above Kr.
Why equity investors demand more: Equity shareholders are residual claimants — they receive whatever is left after all creditors, employees, and preference holders are paid. In a bankruptcy, they may receive nothing. This extreme downside risk demands a commensurately high expected return.

DDM Approach

Zero Growth DDM — No Dividend Growth

The simplest case: dividends are expected to remain constant at D forever (a perpetuity). This is the Gordon Model with g = 0.

If dividends are constant: D₁ = D₂ = D₃ = … = D Price: P₀ = D / Ke Rearranging: Ke = D / P₀ (existing shares — no flotation cost) Ke = D / NP (new issue — NP = P₀ − Flotation Costs) where D = Annual dividend per share (constant forever)

When is zero growth appropriate?

  • Mature, stable companies in regulated industries (utilities, PSUs) where dividends are fixed by policy.
  • Preference shares (which pay a fixed dividend — though called Kp, the formula is identical).
  • As an approximation for very low-growth firms.

Illustration

A company pays a constant dividend of ₹8 per share. Current market price ₹80. Flotation on new issue ₹8.

Ke (existing shares) = D / P₀ = 8/80 = 10.00% Ke (new issue): NP = 80 − 8 = ₹72 Ke = D / NP = 8/72 = 11.11% Interpretation: Existing shareholders earn 10% on their investment; new shares cost 11.11% due to flotation reducing proceeds.

Quick Calculator — Zero Growth

Zero growth Ke = Dividend Yield
Since there is no capital appreciation (no growth), the entire return comes from dividends. Ke equals the current dividend yield — the ratio of dividend to price.
Teaching note: Contrast with the Gordon Model — zero growth means the share price never changes (P₀ = P₁ = P₂), so the investor's return is purely dividend income. The moment g > 0, there is also capital appreciation.
DDM Approach

Gordon Model — Constant Growth DDM

The Gordon Constant Growth Model is the most widely used DDM variant in Indian FM examinations. It assumes dividends grow at a constant rate g forever.

The current price is the PV of all future dividends: P₀ = D₁/(1+Ke) + D₁(1+g)/(1+Ke)² + D₁(1+g)²/(1+Ke)³ + … This infinite geometric series converges to: P₀ = D₁ / (Ke − g) [requires Ke > g] Rearranging to get cost of equity: Ke = D₁/P₀ + g (existing shares, no flotation) Ke = D₁/NP + g (new issue, with flotation cost) where: D₁ = D₀ × (1 + g) (next year's expected dividend) D₀ = Current / most recent dividend paid g = Constant annual growth rate of dividends P₀ = Current market price per share NP = Net Proceeds = P₀ − Flotation Costs per share

Interpretation: two-part return

Ke = Dividend Yield + Capital Gain Yield
D₁/P₀ = the expected dividend yield (income return)
g = the expected rate of capital appreciation (growth in share price)

In a constant-growth world, the share price grows at exactly g — so the capital gain yield = g. The total return Ke = dividend yield + g.

Illustration

Share price ₹150. Most recent dividend ₹9. Growth rate 8% p.a. New issue flotation ₹12.

D₁ = 9 × 1.08 = ₹9.72 Ke (existing shares / Kr): = D₁/P₀ + g = 9.72/150 + 0.08 = 6.48% + 8.00% = 14.48% Ke (new issue): NP = 150 − 12 = ₹138 = D₁/NP + g = 9.72/138 + 0.08 = 7.04% + 8.00% = 15.04%
Key condition: Ke > g
The Gordon Model only works when Ke > g. If g ≥ Ke, the formula gives a negative or undefined price — economically, a stock cannot grow faster than the discount rate forever.

Sensitivity to g

At D₁=₹10, P₀=₹100: if g rises from 5% to 7%, Ke rises from 15% to 17% — the same dividend yield but higher total return requirement.

DDM Approach

Two-Stage Growth DDM

The two-stage model relaxes the constant-growth assumption. It recognises that many firms have two phases: a high-growth phase (years 1 to n) followed by a stable, constant-growth phase (year n+1 onwards forever).

P₀ = Σ[D_t/(1+Ke)ᵗ] + Pₙ/(1+Ke)ⁿ t=1 to n where: D_t = Dividend in year t (growing at g₁ for t = 1..n) Pₙ = Terminal (horizon) price at end of phase 1 = D_(n+1) / (Ke − g₂) g₁ = High growth rate during phase 1 g₂ = Stable (terminal) growth rate from year n+1 Step-by-step procedure: 1. Project dividends D₁ to Dₙ using g₁ 2. Compute terminal price: Pₙ = Dₙ×(1+g₂)/(Ke−g₂) 3. Discount all cash flows back at Ke 4. Set sum = P₀ and solve for Ke by trial and error / IRR

Illustration

D₀ = ₹5. High growth g₁ = 20% for 3 years, then stable g₂ = 7% forever. P₀ = ₹120. Find Ke.

Phase 1 dividends: D₁ = 5×1.20 = ₹6.00 D₂ = 6×1.20 = ₹7.20 D₃ = 7.20×1.20 = ₹8.64 Terminal price at Year 3: P₃ = D₄/(Ke−g₂) = 8.64×1.07/(Ke−0.07) = 9.24/(Ke−0.07) Set P₀ = PV(D₁)+PV(D₂)+PV(D₃)+PV(P₃) = ₹120 Solve for Ke by trial: Ke ≈ 14.8% (solved numerically)

The two-stage model is more realistic but requires solving for Ke by trial and error (it is the IRR of the cash flow stream). The calculator uses numerical bisection for an exact result.
DDM Approach

Estimating the Growth Rate (g)

The growth rate g in the Gordon Model is the most sensitive and difficult input to estimate. Three methods are commonly used:

Method 1 — Sustainable Growth Rate (Gordon's formula)

g = b × ROE where: b = Retention ratio = 1 − Dividend Payout Ratio = (EPS − DPS) / EPS ROE = Return on Equity = Net Profit / Shareholders Equity Intuition: A firm retains a fraction b of its earnings and reinvests it at a return of ROE. The growth in equity (and therefore dividends, assuming constant payout) = b × ROE. Example: EPS = ₹20, DPS = ₹8, Net Worth = ₹200 per share b = (20−8)/20 = 12/20 = 0.60 ROE = 20/200 = 10% g = 0.60 × 10% = 6%

Method 2 — Historical Dividend CAGR

g = (Dₙ / D₀)^(1/n) − 1 where Dₙ = dividend n years ago, D₀ = current dividend Example: Dividends over 4 years: ₹4, ₹5, ₹6, ₹7, ₹8 g = (8/4)^(1/4) − 1 = (2)^0.25 − 1 = 1.189−1 = 18.9% Or using the last and first: g = (8/4)^(1/4)−1

Method 3 — Analyst / Forecasted Growth Rate

Use equity research analyst consensus forecasts for EPS / dividend growth — practical but requires access to research databases.

Growth Rate Calculator

Exam tip: When the question gives EPS, DPS, and book value per share — use g = b×ROE. When it gives a history of dividends — use the CAGR formula. When it explicitly states the growth rate — use it directly.
DDM Approach

Flotation Costs — Effect on Ke

Flotation costs (underwriting, brokerage, legal, printing fees) reduce the net proceeds from a new equity issue below the current market price, raising the effective cost of new equity above the cost of existing equity / retained earnings.

With flotation cost f (₹ per share): NP = P₀ − f With flotation cost f% (percentage of issue price): NP = P₀ × (1 − f%) Effect on Ke: Ke (existing) = D₁/P₀ + g (uses P₀) Ke (new) = D₁/NP + g (uses NP < P₀) → Ke (new) > Ke (existing) always

Detailed illustration

P₀ = ₹200, D₀ = ₹10, g = 6%, Flotation = 5% of market price.

D₁ = 10 × 1.06 = ₹10.60 NP = 200 × (1 − 0.05) = 200 × 0.95 = ₹190 Ke (existing / Kr) = 10.60/200 + 0.06 = 5.30% + 6% = 11.30% Ke (new issue) = 10.60/190 + 0.06 = 5.58% + 6% = 11.58% Flotation premium = 11.58% − 11.30% = 0.28 percentage points At scale: for ₹10 crore new equity issue, this 0.28% difference costs ₹2.8 lakh per year in extra cost of capital.
Pecking Order implication: Because flotation costs make new equity more expensive than retained earnings, firms prefer internal finance (retained earnings) over new equity issuance. This is one of the key insights of the Pecking Order Theory of capital structure.

Ke rises as flotation cost % increases. The gap between Ke (new) and Kr widens with higher flotation costs.

Two methods to handle flotation in project evaluation:
Method 1 (Adjust Ke): Use NP instead of P₀ → higher Ke → higher WACC → lower NPV
Method 2 (Adjust C₀): Add flotation costs to project's initial outlay; use unadjusted Ke in WACC

Method 2 is theoretically preferred — flotation is a one-time cash outflow, not a permanent increase in cost.
DDM Approach

Cost of Retained Earnings (Kr) vs Cost of New Equity (Ke)

Retained earnings are profits NOT paid as dividends. They belong to equity shareholders — who could have received them and invested elsewhere at Ke. Retaining them imposes an opportunity cost of Kr on the firm.

Kr = D₁/P₀ + g (no flotation cost — no new issue) Ke = D₁/NP + g (with flotation cost — NP < P₀) Since NP < P₀: D₁/NP > D₁/P₀ → Ke > Kr always Kr is the cost of using existing equity funds (retained earnings). Ke is the cost of raising new equity (fresh issue of shares). Kr = Ke only when flotation costs are zero.

Three approaches to estimate Kr

ApproachFormulaLogic
Opportunity Cost (most common)Kr = D₁/P₀ + gShareholders could earn Ke on equivalent-risk investments
CAPMKr = Rf + β×(Rm−Rf)Same systematic risk as equity; no new issue cost
External Yield CriterionKr = Ke (external)Use new equity Ke as upper bound; conservatively treat Kr = Ke
In WACC — which to use? Use Kr for the equity already in the firm's balance sheet (retained earnings, reserves). Use Ke (with flotation) only if the firm is actively raising new equity. In most WACC problems, unless new equity is specified, use Kr = D₁/P₀ + g with the current market price.

Illustration

P₀=₹100, D₀=₹6, g=8%, Flotation=₹7 per share.

D₁ = 6 × 1.08 = ₹6.48 Kr = 6.48/100 + 0.08 = 6.48% + 8% = 14.48% Ke (new) = 6.48/(100−7) + 0.08 = 6.48/93 + 0.08 = 6.97% + 8% = 14.97% Kr < Ke (new): 14.48% < 14.97% Difference = 0.49% due to flotation cost

Why retained earnings are not free

Opportunity cost argument: If the firm retained ₹100 of profits, shareholders forgo the opportunity to invest that ₹100 elsewhere at an equivalent rate of return (Ke = 14.48%). Retaining earnings therefore has an implicit cost — even though no cheque is written.
Teaching note: Students often think retained earnings are free. Emphasise: retained earnings are not owned by the firm — they belong to shareholders. The cost is the return shareholders sacrifice by not receiving dividends.
CAPM Approach

Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

CAPM is the most theoretically rigorous method for estimating Ke. It recognises that investors in a well-diversified portfolio only care about systematic (market) risk — risk that cannot be diversified away. This risk is measured by Beta (β).

CAPM: Ke = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf) = Rf + β × MRP where: Ke = Required return on equity (cost of equity) Rf = Risk-free rate (yield on govt. T-bills / bonds) β (Beta) = Systematic risk of the stock β > 1 → more volatile than market (aggressive) β = 1 → moves exactly with the market β < 1 → less volatile (defensive) β = 0 → risk-free asset (no systematic risk) Rm = Expected return on the market portfolio MRP = Market Risk Premium = Rm − Rf

The logic of CAPM

  • Investors can eliminate unsystematic (firm-specific) risk through diversification — so they receive no compensation for it.
  • Investors cannot diversify away systematic (market) risk — they demand a premium for bearing it.
  • Beta measures how much systematic risk a stock has. Higher Beta → higher required return.
  • The required return rises linearly with Beta — this relationship is the Security Market Line (SML).

CAPM — Illustration

Rf = 7%, Rm = 14%, β = 1.3.

MRP = 14% − 7% = 7% Ke = 7% + 1.3 × 7% = 7% + 9.1% = 16.1% Interpretation: A stock with β = 1.3 (30% more volatile than the market) must offer a return of 16.1% to compensate investors for its systematic risk. At β = 0: Ke = 7% = Rf (risk-free) At β = 1: Ke = 14% = Rm (market return) At β = 1.3: Ke = 16.1% (above-market return)
CAPM vs DDM: CAPM gives a Ke based purely on risk (Beta). DDM gives a Ke based on price and dividend expectations. They often give different results — both are valid; using both and averaging is common in practice.
CAPM Approach

Security Market Line (SML)

The SML is the graphical representation of CAPM — a straight line plotting the required return (Ke) against Beta (β). Every asset correctly priced in equilibrium lies exactly on the SML.

SML equation: Ke = Rf + β × MRP This is a straight line with: y-intercept = Rf (at β = 0: risk-free return) slope = MRP = Rm − Rf (each unit of β adds MRP to Ke) At β = 0: Ke = Rf At β = 1: Ke = Rm For β > 1: Ke > Rm (aggressive stock — more than market risk) For β < 1: Rf < Ke < Rm (defensive stock)

Assets above and below the SML

Asset above SML (Ke actual > SML Ke):
Undervalued — offering more return than required for its risk. Investors buy → price rises → Ke falls → asset moves back to SML.
Asset below SML (Ke actual < SML Ke):
Overvalued — offering less return than required. Investors sell → price falls → Ke rises → asset moves back to SML.
Shifts in the SML:
• If Rf rises (e.g., RBI hikes rates) → SML shifts up parallel → all Ke values rise
• If MRP rises (increased market risk aversion) → SML rotates upward (steeper) → higher-Beta stocks are more affected
• Individual stock's Beta changes → stock moves along the SML
CAPM Approach

Beta (β) — Systematic Risk Explained

Beta measures the sensitivity of a stock's returns to market returns — the degree to which the stock moves with the overall market.

β = Cov(Rᵢ, Rₘ) / Var(Rₘ) = ρᵢₘ × σᵢ / σₘ where: Cov(Rᵢ, Rₘ) = Covariance of stock with market Var(Rₘ) = Variance of market return ρᵢₘ = Correlation between stock and market σᵢ = Standard deviation of stock returns σₘ = Standard deviation of market returns

Beta interpretation table

Beta ValueTypeMeaningExample sectors
β < 0InverseMoves opposite to market (very rare)Gold in some markets
β = 0Risk-freeNo systematic riskT-bills, cash
0 < β < 1DefensiveLess volatile than marketFMCG, utilities, pharma
β = 1MarketMoves exactly with marketIndex fund / ETF
β > 1AggressiveMore volatile than marketBanks, metals, tech, real estate

Portfolio Beta

β_portfolio = Σ(wᵢ × βᵢ) where wᵢ = weight of stock i in the portfolio Example: 60% in stock A (β=1.4) + 40% in stock B (β=0.8): β_portfolio = 0.60×1.4 + 0.40×0.8 = 0.84 + 0.32 = 1.16

CAPM Approach

Levered vs Unlevered Beta — Adjusting Beta for Capital Structure

A firm's observed (levered) Beta (βₗ) reflects both its business risk AND financial risk (from debt). When comparing firms with different capital structures, or when using a proxy firm's Beta for a different target structure, we need to strip out the financial risk effect using the Hamada Equation.

Hamada Equation (with corporate tax): βₗ = βᵤ × [1 + (1−Tc) × D/E] Unlevering (removing financial risk): βᵤ = βₗ / [1 + (1−Tc) × D/E] Re-levering (applying new D/E ratio): βₗ_new = βᵤ × [1 + (1−Tc) × D_new/E_new] where: βₗ = Observed (levered) Beta — reflects total risk βᵤ = Unlevered (asset) Beta — pure business risk Tc = Corporate tax rate D/E = Debt-to-Equity ratio (market values)

Practical application — Pure Play Method

When a firm enters a new industry and needs Ke for a project, it finds a pure-play comparable firm in that industry, unlever the comparable's Beta, then re-lever at its own target D/E to get the project Beta.

Step 1: Find comparable firm's βₗ_comp Step 2: Unlever: βᵤ = βₗ_comp / [1+(1−Tc)×(D/E)_comp] Step 3: Re-lever: βₗ_target = βᵤ × [1+(1−Tc)×(D/E)_target] Step 4: Apply CAPM: Ke = Rf + βₗ_target × MRP

Teaching note: The difficulty of estimating these inputs is the main practical limitation of CAPM. In Indian exams, inputs are always given — the challenge is the formula and interpretation. In practice, analysts use market data services (Bloomberg, Capitaline) for Beta and published G-Sec yields for Rf.

Limitations of CAPM

Beta is estimated from historical data but used to predict future returns — Beta is not stable over time.
Assumes a single factor (market risk) drives returns — ignores size, value, momentum effects (Fama-French).
Requires a well-diversified market portfolio — in practice, market indices are imperfect proxies.
The risk-free rate is not truly risk-free (government bonds carry inflation risk).
Assumes investors have homogeneous expectations — not realistic.
Other Methods

Bond Yield Plus Risk Premium Method

The simplest approach to estimating Ke: add a judgemental equity risk premium to the firm's own cost of long-term debt (yield on its bonds).

Ke = YTM on firm's long-term debt + Equity Risk Premium Equity Risk Premium = 3% to 6% (typical range — judgemental) Example: A firm's 10-year debentures yield 11% (pre-tax). Ke = 11% + 4% = 15% Or using after-tax Kd as base: Ke = Kd(after-tax) + Equity Risk Premium (adjusted)

Rationale

The firm's own bond yield captures its credit risk and the current level of interest rates. Since equity is riskier than debt (residual claim), equity investors must earn more — the additional return is the equity risk premium.

Advantages and Disadvantages

Advantages: Simple and quick; uses current market data (bond yield); useful when dividend history is erratic or Beta is unreliable.
Disadvantages: The risk premium is entirely subjective — different analysts will add different premiums. Less theoretically grounded than CAPM. Not useful for firms with no publicly traded bonds.

Bond Yield + Risk Premium Calculator

When to use this method:
Use when a firm has publicly traded bonds but either pays no dividends (making DDM impossible) or is newly listed (making Beta estimation unreliable). It provides a quick sanity check on CAPM and DDM estimates.

Growth Rate (g) Calculator

Apply g to Gordon Model

Example 1 — Zero Growth Ke

A company pays a constant annual dividend of ₹12 per share. Current market price ₹96. Flotation on new issue ₹8. Compute Ke for existing shares and new issue.

Ke (existing / Kr) = D/P₀ = 12/96 = 12.50%

NP = 96 − 8 = ₹88

Ke (new issue) = D/NP = 12/88 = 13.64%

Difference of 1.14% purely due to flotation cost reducing net proceeds.

Example 2 — Gordon Model with g

Share price ₹180. Last dividend ₹9. Growth rate 8%. Flotation on new issue ₹15. Compute Kr (retained earnings) and Ke (new issue).

D₁ = 9 × 1.08 = ₹9.72

Kr = 9.72/180 + 0.08 = 5.40% + 8% = 13.40%

NP = 180 − 15 = ₹165

Ke = 9.72/165 + 0.08 = 5.89% + 8% = 13.89%

Example 3 — Estimating g and then Ke

EPS = ₹30, DPS = ₹12, Book Value per share = ₹250. Market price ₹300. Compute g (using b×ROE) and then Ke.

b = (30−12)/30 = 18/30 = 0.60

ROE = EPS/BV = 30/250 = 12%

g = b × ROE = 0.60 × 12% = 7.20%

D₁ = 12 × 1.072 = ₹12.864

Ke = 12.864/300 + 0.072 = 4.29% + 7.20% = 11.49%

Example 4 — CAPM

Rf = 7.5%, Rm = 15%, Beta = 1.4. Compute Ke using CAPM. Also compute Ke if Beta falls to 0.9 (after the firm deleverages).

MRP = 15% − 7.5% = 7.5%

Ke (β=1.4) = 7.5% + 1.4 × 7.5% = 7.5% + 10.5% = 18.0%

Ke (β=0.9) = 7.5% + 0.9 × 7.5% = 7.5% + 6.75% = 14.25%

Reducing Beta from 1.4 to 0.9 (deleveraging) reduces Ke by 3.75% — a significant reduction in cost of equity capital.

Example 5 — Hamada Equation (Beta Adjustment)

Comparable firm: βₗ = 1.6, D/E = 0.8, Tax 30%. Target firm: D/E = 0.4. Rf = 7%, Rm = 13%. Compute target firm's Ke.

Unlever: βᵤ = 1.6 / [1 + (1−0.30) × 0.8] = 1.6 / [1 + 0.56] = 1.6/1.56 = 1.026

Re-lever at D/E=0.4: βₗ_target = 1.026 × [1 + 0.70×0.4] = 1.026 × 1.28 = 1.313

Ke = 7% + 1.313 × (13%−7%) = 7% + 1.313×6% = 7% + 7.88% = 14.88%

Example 6 — DDM vs CAPM Comparison

P₀ = ₹200, D₀ = ₹10, g = 7%. Rf = 8%, Rm = 15%, β = 1.1. Compute Ke by both methods and comment.

DDM: D₁ = 10×1.07 = ₹10.70; Ke = 10.70/200 + 0.07 = 5.35% + 7% = 12.35%

CAPM: MRP = 7%; Ke = 8% + 1.1×7% = 8% + 7.7% = 15.70%

DDM gives 12.35%; CAPM gives 15.70% — a significant divergence of 3.35%. Possible reasons: the assumed growth rate g=7% may be too optimistic (overvaluing the stock), or the stock's Beta may overstate its true systematic risk. In practice, analysts would investigate this divergence before selecting a Ke estimate, or use the average: (12.35+15.70)/2 = 14.03%.

Formula Bank — Cost of Equity

Zero Growth DDM

Ke = D / P₀ (existing shares) Ke = D / NP (new issue; NP = P₀ − flotation)

Gordon Constant Growth DDM

D₁ = D₀ × (1 + g) Kr = D₁/P₀ + g (retained earnings / existing) Ke = D₁/NP + g (new equity issue) NP = P₀ − Flotation Cost

Growth Rate Estimation

g = b × ROE b = 1 − DPS/EPS = Retention Ratio ROE = EPS / Book Value per share g (CAGR) = (Dₙ/D₀)^(1/n) − 1

CAPM

Ke = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf) = Rf + β × MRP MRP = Rm − Rf SML: y-intercept = Rf; slope = MRP

Beta

β = Cov(Ri,Rm) / Var(Rm) = ρ × σᵢ/σₘ β_portfolio = Σ(wᵢ × βᵢ)

Hamada Equation

βₗ = βᵤ × [1 + (1−Tc) × D/E] βᵤ = βₗ / [1 + (1−Tc) × D/E]

Bond Yield + Risk Premium

Ke = YTM on bonds + Equity Risk Premium

Cost Hierarchy

Kd(after-tax) < Kp < Kr < Ke (new issue)
