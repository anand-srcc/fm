Foundations

What is the Cost of Capital?

The cost of capital is the minimum rate of return a firm must earn on its investments to satisfy the expectations of its fund providers — debt holders, preference shareholders, and equity shareholders. It is simultaneously:

  • The required rate of return that capital suppliers demand as compensation for the time value of money and the risk they bear.
  • The cut-off rate (hurdle rate) for capital budgeting — accept projects only if their return exceeds the cost of capital.
  • The discount rate used to find the present value of future cash flows in NPV analysis.
  • The opportunity cost of funds — money invested here cannot earn elsewhere; investors must be compensated at least at the market rate for equivalent risk.
Why it matters. An incorrect cost of capital leads to wrong investment decisions. Overestimating it rejects value-creating projects; underestimating it accepts value-destroying ones. Getting WACC right is therefore central to corporate value creation.

Risk-free rate of return
The foundation. Usually approximated by Government of India T-bill or G-sec yield.
Business risk
Variability in operating income; influences Ke and WACC through beta and credit spread.
Financial risk
Additional risk from using debt; raises Ke (shareholders demand more) and may raise Kd.

Influences the DGM estimate of Ke through g (growth rate).
Conceptual Foundation

The Concept of Required Return & Opportunity Cost

Required rate of return

Capital providers are risk-averse. They will supply funds only if their expected return at least matches what they could earn elsewhere for the same risk level. This is the required rate of return — it is market-determined, not decided by the firm.

Required Return = Risk-free Rate + Risk Premium Kᵢ = Rƒ + β × (Rm − Rƒ) [CAPM form] = Rƒ + Business-risk premium + Financial-risk premium

Opportunity cost

From the firm's perspective, funds already raised have an opportunity cost: they could be returned to investors who would reinvest elsewhere. Even retained earnings cost the firm, because those rupees belong to shareholders who forgo returns on alternative investments.

Using retained earnings ≠ "free money." The opportunity cost of equity = what shareholders could earn on an investment of equivalent risk. Ignoring this systematically underestimates cost of capital.

Three perspectives on cost of capital

Investor perspective — Minimum return needed to justify the investment given its risk. Below this, investors sell, driving down the stock price.
Firm perspective — Hurdle rate for new investments. Projects earning above WACC add value; below WACC destroy value.
Market equilibrium — In efficient markets, price adjusts so actual return = required return. A firm can raise capital at its WACC because the market prices the securities fairly.

Specific cost vs composite cost

Specific (component) cost is the cost of a single source of finance — Kd, Kp, Ke or Kr — computed individually. Composite cost (WACC) is the weighted average of all specific costs, reflecting the firm's overall capital structure. For investment decisions the WACC is the correct discount rate unless the project changes the firm's risk or leverage.

Historical vs Marginal cost

Historical (embedded) cost
Cost of funds already raised. Useful for evaluating past financing decisions. Not the right rate for new investment decisions.
Marginal cost
Cost of the next rupee raised. Relevant for new investments. Rises as cheaper sources exhaust (see MCC Schedule). Always use marginal cost for capital budgeting.
Component Cost

Cost of Debt (Kd)

Debt (debentures, bonds, term loans) carries contractual interest. Because interest is tax-deductible, the firm's effective (after-tax) cost of debt is lower than the coupon rate. This tax shield makes debt attractive up to a point.

1. Perpetual / irredeemable debt

Kd (before tax) = Annual interest / Net proceeds Kd (after tax) = Kd (before tax) × (1 − Tax rate) = [I / NP] × (1 − T)

2. Redeemable debt

Kd (approx.) = [I(1−T) + (RV − NP)/n] / [(RV + NP)/2] where: I = Annual interest = Face value × Coupon rate T = Corporate tax rate RV = Redemption value (par or at premium/discount) NP = Net proceeds (issue price less flotation costs) n = Years to maturity

Derivation — why approximate formula works

Exact Kd is the YTM (after-tax) solving: NP = Σ[I(1−T)/(1+r)ᵗ] + RV/(1+r)ⁿ iteratively. The approximate formula linearises the capital gain/loss over n years and averages book value — it gives Kd within ±30–50 bps for typical maturities. Always use the exact (IRR) method when precision matters.

3. Zero-coupon bonds

Kd (before tax) = (RV / Issue price)^(1/n) − 1 After-tax Kd applies only if amortised discount is tax-deductible (check jurisdiction).

4. Floating-rate debt

Use expected future short-term rate (e.g. MCLR + spread) or the forward rate implied by the yield curve. Then apply (1−T).

Advantages of using debt
Tax shield on interest lowers effective cost. Fixed obligations — shareholders retain upside. Generally cheapest explicit source of funds (less risky to holder).
Risks of debt
Increases financial risk (interest coverage ratio falls). Too much debt raises both Kd (credit spread widens) and Ke (shareholders demand higher return). Beyond optimal D/E, WACC rises.
Teaching note: Contrast pre-tax Kd vs after-tax Kd numerically. A 10% coupon at 30% tax → after-tax cost = 7%. The ₹3 per ₹100 saved by the government makes debt appear cheaper — the real cost is lower, but so is the firm's interest cover.

Component Cost

Cost of Preference Shares (Kp)

Preference shareholders receive a fixed dividend before equity holders. Unlike debt interest, preference dividends are not tax-deductible — they are paid from post-tax profits. This makes preference capital more expensive than debt on an after-tax basis for the same coupon level.

1. Irredeemable preference shares

Kp = Preference dividend per share / Net proceeds per share = D / NP

2. Redeemable preference shares

Kp (approx.) = [D + (RV − NP)/n] / [(RV + NP)/2] where: D = Annual preference dividend (fixed) RV = Redemption value NP = Net proceeds (issue price − flotation cost) n = Years to redemption

Why no tax adjustment for Kp?

Interest expense reduces taxable income → government shares part of debt cost via tax saving. Dividends (preference or equity) are paid from profit after tax — no deduction allowed. Hence Kp formula has no (1−T) factor. Numerically: 10% preference shares vs 10% debentures at 30% tax → Kp = 10%, Kd (AT) = 7%. Preference is 43% more expensive after tax.

3. Cumulative vs non-cumulative

For cost estimation, cumulative arrear dividends are treated as certain (must be paid before equity). Non-cumulative missed dividends are lost, so cost calculation may need probability-weighted dividends — rare in practice, use stated rate as base.

Advantages
Fixed cost — below equity in uncertainty; no dilution of EPS. Provides financial leverage (like debt) without formal bankruptcy risk.
Limitations
No tax shield → expensive relative to debt. Missed dividends accumulate (cumulative type) and restrict equity dividends. Treated as quasi-debt by rating agencies, constraining further borrowing.
Teaching note: Compare Kp and Kd numerically — students often forget the tax adjustment. Ask: "What coupon on debt equals a 12% preference share cost?" Answer: 12%/(1−T). At T=30%, need 17.14% coupon on debt.

Component Cost · Most Debated

Cost of Equity (Ke) — New Issue

Equity is the most difficult component to estimate because there is no contractual return — shareholders expect a risk-appropriate return that is unobservable. Three main models are used:

Model 1 — Dividend Growth Model (Gordon)

Ke = D₁/P₀ + g = D₀(1+g)/[P₀(1−f)] + g D₁ = next expected dividend P₀ = current market price f = flotation cost (%) g = constant growth rate in dividends = RoE × retention ratio (b) = ROE × (1−payout ratio)

Derivation

From Gordon's price model: P₀ = D₁/(Ke − g) → solving for Ke gives D₁/P₀ + g. For new equity, proceeds = P₀(1−f), so Ke = D₁/[P₀(1−f)] + g. This is the dividend-yield plus growth approach.

When to use: Firm pays stable dividends with predictable growth. Works well for mature, dividend-paying companies (e.g. FMCG, utilities).
Limitation: Circular (g depends on future ROE). Fails for zero-dividend or high-growth firms. Sensitive to g estimate.

Model 2 — Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

Ke = Rƒ + β × (Rm − Rƒ) Rƒ = risk-free rate (G-sec yield) β = systematic risk (beta) of the stock Rm = expected market return (Rm−Rƒ) = equity risk premium (ERP) For levered β: βL = βU × [1 + (1−T)(D/E)]

Derivation

CAPM equilibrium: in a diversified portfolio, only systematic (market) risk is priced. Beta measures sensitivity of stock return to market return (covariance with market / market variance). Required return is linear in beta: every additional unit of beta demands (Rm−Rƒ) in extra return.

When to use: Market data available; beta estimable from historical returns. Preferred for listed companies with traded equity.
Limitation: Beta is backward-looking; ERP estimation is subjective; single-factor model (Fama-French adds size, value).

Model 3 — Earnings / Price (E/P) Approach

Ke = EPS / Market price per share = E₁ / P₀ Or with growth: Ke = E₁ / P₀ + g Or earnings-yield on book value: Ke = Return on equity (ROE)

Rationale

If the firm retains all earnings and reinvests at ROE, the stock's required return must equal ROE for the price to be fair. This model ignores dividend policy but captures total return to shareholders including retained profits. Best used as a cross-check against DGM and CAPM.

When to use: Zero-dividend firms; as a quick cross-check. Related to justified P/E ratio.
Limitation: EPS is accounting-based, not cash-based. Does not separate dividends from capital gains.

Component Cost

Cost of Retained Earnings (Kr)

Retained earnings belong to existing shareholders. If the firm retains profits instead of paying them out, shareholders forgo dividends. The cost of retained earnings is therefore the opportunity cost — what shareholders could earn on an investment of equivalent risk. It equals the cost of new equity minus flotation costs (since retained earnings need not be raised externally).

Three approaches

Opportunity cost / DGM approach
Kr = D₁/P₀ + g (no flotation cost deduction)
Shareholders can reinvest dividends in the market at this rate — so the firm must match it.
CAPM approach
Kr = Rƒ + β × (Rm − Rƒ)
Beta captures systematic risk. No difference from Ke (CAPM) — CAPM gives no flotation cost distinction.
Bond-yield-plus-risk-premium
Kr = Kd (pre-tax) + Equity risk premium
Quick estimate. Risk premium is typically 3–5% added to the firm's own bond yield.

Kr vs Ke — key difference

Kr (Retained earnings)Ke (New equity issue)
SourceInternal (no new issue)External (fresh equity issue)
Flotation costNone — no transactionYes — underwriting, brokerage
Formula (DGM)D₁/P₀ + gD₁/[P₀(1−f)] + g
MagnitudeLower (or equal) than KeHigher than Kr
When flotation costs are zero, Kr = Ke. In practice Ke > Kr because flotation costs inflate the denominator of the DGM formula for new issues.

The shareholder preference argument

Some argue Kr should be adjusted for personal taxes shareholders pay on dividends. If shareholders face a higher marginal tax rate on dividends than on capital gains, the opportunity cost of retained earnings (keeping funds inside the firm to generate capital gains) may actually be lower than Ke. This is the basis of Litzenberger–Ramaswamy's tax-adjusted cost of equity. In Indian context, dividends now taxed at slab rates (post FY21), making this particularly relevant.

Teaching note: Pose this question: "A firm has ₹50 Cr of retained earnings this year. Should it treat these as free funds?" Discussion should surface the opportunity cost concept — retained earnings are the shareholders' money, placed in the firm's hands as trustees. Misuse leads to value destruction.

Issue Costs

Flotation Costs — Impact on Component Costs

Flotation costs are the expenses of issuing new securities: underwriting commission, brokerage, legal fees, SEBI filing charges, printing, and advertising. They reduce the net proceeds received by the firm, raising the effective cost of capital.

Two treatment approaches

Approach 1 — Adjust net proceeds (textbook method)

Ke = D₁ / [P₀ × (1−f)] + g Kp = [Div + (RV−NP)/n] / [(RV+NP)/2] where NP = Issue price × (1−f) Kd = [I(1−T) + (RV−NP)/n] / [(RV+NP)/2]

Flotation cost is spread over the life of the security via the denominator. Simplest, most widely used in Indian financial management courses.

Approach 2 — Flotation-adjusted NPV (Brealey-Myers)

Adjusted NPV = Base NPV − PV of flotation costs Or: raise the hurdle rate so that: Cost per rupee raised = 1/(1−f) Effective Ke = Ke/(1−f) [Conservative — over-adjusts for long-lived projects]

More theoretically correct but less commonly tested in Indian university exams. Approach 1 is standard for most problems.

Typical flotation rates in India

Security TypeFlotation Cost Range
Equity (IPO / FPO)3% – 8% of issue price
Rights issue1% – 3%
Preference shares2% – 5%
Debentures (public issue)1% – 3%
Private placement0.5% – 1%

Worked illustration

Example — Effect of flotation on Ke

D₀ = ₹10, g = 8%, P₀ = ₹150, f = 5%

D₁ = 10 × 1.08 = ₹10.80

Without flotation: Ke = 10.80/150 + 0.08 = 7.2% + 8% = 15.2%

With flotation (net proceeds = 150 × 0.95 = ₹142.50):
Ke = 10.80/142.50 + 0.08 = 7.58% + 8% = 15.58%

Flotation increases Ke by 38 basis points — seemingly small but significant at scale.

Core Composite Measure

Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

WACC is the overall cost of capital, calculated as the weighted average of the costs of each component of the capital structure. Weights reflect the proportion of each source in total financing.

WACC = Wₑ×Kₑ + W_p×K_p + W_d×K_d(1−T) + W_r×K_r where: Wₑ = Weight of equity (new issue) Kₑ = Cost of new equity W_p = Weight of preference shares K_p = Cost of preference W_d = Weight of debt K_d = Pre-tax cost of debt W_r = Weight of retained earnings K_r = Cost of retained earnings T = Corporate tax rate All weights sum to 1.0

Derivation of WACC from asset valuation

The firm's total market value V = D + E. The firm's assets generate operating cash flows (EBIT). Equity holders receive EBIT − I(1−T) as after-tax earnings; debt holders receive I(1−T) in after-tax equivalent terms adjusted for the tax shield. In a Modigliani-Miller world, WACC = r_A (unlevered cost of assets) when ignoring taxes. With corporate tax: WACC = r_U × (1 − T×D/V). This confirms WACC falls with leverage (up to a point) due to the tax shield — the foundation of optimal capital structure.

Decision rule

Accept a project if its expected IRR (or risk-adjusted return) exceeds WACC. WACC is the minimum required return on assets funded by the firm's capital structure mix. If IRR > WACC, the project adds value; if IRR < WACC, it destroys value.

Conditions for WACC to be the correct discount rate

  • The new project has the same business risk as the firm's existing assets (if not, adjust β).
  • The new project is financed in the same proportions as the current capital structure.
  • The capital structure is assumed to be optimal (or at its target).
  • The project does not affect the firm's overall risk perception in the market.

Common errors

ErrorConsequence
Using book weights instead of market weightsUnderweights equity (usually), biases WACC

WACC Weights

Book Weights vs Market Weights vs Target Weights

Book (accounting) weights

Based on balance sheet values — paid-up share capital, reserves, debenture book value. Readily available but can differ significantly from market values, especially for equity. Equity book value rarely reflects market price. Understates equity weight in most bull markets.

Market value weights

Market capitalisation for equity (shares × CMP), market value of bonds (YTM-based), face value for preference if illiquid. Theoretically preferred — captures today's opportunity costs. But fluctuates daily with stock prices, making WACC volatile.

Target (optimal) weights

The proportions the firm plans to maintain at its optimal capital structure. Most appropriate for long-run investment decisions — stable, forward-looking, reflects the firm's financing intent. Used by CFOs for strategic planning. Difficult to estimate if no explicit target exists.

Dynamic Cost of Capital

Marginal Cost of Capital (MCC)

The marginal cost of capital (also called the incremental cost of capital) is the cost of obtaining an additional rupee of new capital. As a firm raises more funds, it exhausts cheaper sources first — retained earnings before new equity, lower-cost debt tranches before high-yield ones. Beyond each break point, the WACC rises.

Why MCC rises

  • Retained earnings are limited — once exhausted, the firm must issue new equity (higher Ke due to flotation costs).
  • Debt capacity limits — lenders demand higher interest rates as leverage rises (credit spread widens).
  • Signalling — large equity issues may signal overvaluation, depressing price and raising effective Ke.
  • Agency costs — more debt increases monitoring costs, raising effective Kd.

Break point formula

Break Point = Limit of cheaper source / Weight of that source BP_equity = Available retained earnings / Wₑ BP_debt = Maximum low-cost debt / W_d Beyond a break point, the WACC schedule steps up.

Investment opportunity schedule (IOS)

Projects ranked by return (IRR) from highest to lowest, plotted against cumulative investment. Where MCC intersects IOS is the optimal capital budget — accept all projects whose IRR exceeds the MCC applicable at that funding level.

Break Points & MCC Schedule — Step-by-Step

How to draw the MCC schedule

1. List all capital sources with costs at each tranche→ 2. Identify limits of cheaper tranches→ 3. Compute break point = Limit / Weight→ 4. Compute WACC for each segment→ 5. Plot horizontal steps against cumulative capital

Break point formula expanded

BP_source = (Amount of lower-cost component) / (Weight of that component) Multiple sources can have break points. Rank all break points in ascending order. Each break point creates a new WACC step (usually higher). Example: if 12% bonds available up to ₹120 Cr and Wd = 0.4: BP_debt = 120/0.4 = ₹300 Cr total fund raised

Equity Cost Model

Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

CAPM establishes a linear relationship between systematic risk (beta) and required return. It is the dominant model for estimating the cost of equity for listed companies with reliable price history.

Ke = Rƒ + β × (Rm − Rƒ) Security Market Line (SML): E(Ri) = Rƒ + [E(Rm) − Rƒ] × βᵢ Decomposition: Rƒ = Time value (compensation for waiting) β × (Rm−Rƒ) = Risk premium (compensation for systematic risk) Capital Market Line (for portfolios): E(Rp) = Rƒ + [(E(Rm)−Rƒ)/σm] × σp

Beta (β) — understanding and estimation

What beta measures

  • β = 1.0 → moves exactly with market
  • β > 1.0 → more volatile than market (aggressive stock)
  • β < 1.0 → less volatile (defensive stock)
  • β = 0 → uncorrelated with market (e.g. T-bill)
  • β < 0 → inverse to market (e.g. gold in some periods)

How to estimate beta

β = Cov(Ri, Rm) / Var(Rm) = [Σ(Ri−R̄i)(Rm−R̄m)] / Σ(Rm−R̄m)² Excel: =SLOPE(stock_returns, market_returns) Or: =COVAR(stock,mkt)/VAR(mkt) Regression: Ri = α + β×Rm + ε

Levered vs Unlevered Beta

Hamada equation: βL = βU × [1 + (1−T) × (D/E)] Unlever: βU = βL / [1 + (1−T)(D/E)] Re-lever: βL = βU × [1 + (1−T)(D/E)] Use case: Estimate βU from a comparable listed firm, then re-lever to your target D/E to get Ke for a private firm or new capital structure.

Worked example — Pure-play method for private firm

You are estimating Ke for an unlisted FMCG company with D/E = 0.6. Find a listed pure-play comparable: Hindustan Unilever (HUL), βL = 0.55, D/E = 0.05, T = 30%.

Step 1 — Unlever HUL: βU = 0.55/[1 + 0.7×0.05] = 0.55/1.035 = 0.531

Step 2 — Re-lever to target D/E: βL = 0.531×[1 + 0.7×0.6] = 0.531×1.42 = 0.754

Step 3 — CAPM: Ke = 6.5 + 0.754×(13−6.5) = 6.5 + 4.9 = 11.4%

Teaching note: Draw the SML on the board. Plot WACC, then plot two projects — one above SML (accept) and one below (reject). This visual immediately clarifies why beta-risk matters for project acceptance, not just accounting return.

Dividend Growth Model (DGM) — Deep Dive

Gordon's Constant Growth Model

P₀ = D₁/(Ke − g) → Ke = D₁/P₀ + g where g is constant forever (stable-growth firm)

Two-stage DGM (supernormal + stable growth)

P₀ = Σ[Dₜ/(1+Ke)ᵗ] + Pn/(1+Ke)ⁿ for t=1 to n Pn = Dn+1/(Ke−g₂) [terminal value at year n] g₁ = high growth rate for years 1–n g₂ = stable growth rate thereafter Ke solved iteratively (trial-and-error or Excel IRR)

Derivation — Gordon growth model

If dividends grow at constant rate g, then Dₜ = D₀(1+g)ᵗ. Stock price is PV of infinite dividend stream: P₀ = Σ D₀(1+g)ᵗ/(1+Ke)ᵗ = D₁/(Ke−g) [geometric series, converges when Ke > g]. Rearranging gives Ke = D₁/P₀ + g. The first term is the "dividend yield component" and g is the "capital gain component."

DGM weaknesses
Circular — g depends on future ROE, which depends on Ke. Cannot be used for zero-dividend or buyback-only firms. Extremely sensitive to g: ±1% in g can shift Ke by 1–2%. Assumes constant growth — unrealistic for cyclical or high-growth firms.

Equity Cost Model

Bond-Yield-Plus-Risk-Premium Approach

This approach estimates the cost of equity by adding a subjective equity risk premium (ERP) to the firm's own long-term pre-tax cost of debt. It is fast, intuitive, and requires no dividend or beta data — useful when those are unavailable or unreliable.

Ke = Pre-tax cost of debt (Kd) + Equity risk premium = YTM of firm's bonds + RP Typical RP range: 3% – 5% in India (Reflects additional risk equity bears vs debt holders)

Rationale

  • Equity holders bear more risk than bondholders — they rank last in liquidation and their return is residual.
  • Higher risk → higher required return → Ke > Kd always.
  • The premium (3–5%) reflects additional business and financial risk borne by equity.
  • This method is commonly used by practitioners and investment bankers as a sanity check.

Advanced Topic

Capital Structure & Its Effect on WACC

The relationship between capital structure (D/E mix) and WACC is the central question of capital structure theory. Does an optimal capital structure exist? If so, where?

Modigliani-Miller (MM) — No taxes

WACC = r_A (constant) V_L = V_U (firm value unchanged)

With perfect capital markets and no taxes, how you slice the financing pie doesn't affect its total size. WACC is constant regardless of leverage. Ke rises with D/E to exactly offset the cheaper debt, leaving WACC unchanged.

Proposition I: V_L = V_U — leverage is irrelevant to firm value in perfect markets.
Proposition II: Ke = r_A + (r_A − Kd)×(D/E) — Ke rises linearly with leverage.

MM — With corporate tax

V_L = V_U + T×D WACC_L = r_A × (1 − T×D/V) PV(tax shield) = T × D (perpetual debt)

Corporate tax creates a debt tax shield — interest is deductible, so the government effectively subsidises debt. Each rupee of debt adds T rupees of value. This implies 100% debt is optimal — clearly unrealistic, pointing to missing costs (distress, agency).

Trade-off Theory (optimal structure)

V_L = V_U + PV(tax shield) − PV(distress costs) Optimal D/E: marginal tax shield = marginal distress cost

Real-world optimal capital structure balances the tax shield benefit of debt against the rising costs of financial distress and agency conflicts. WACC has a minimum (U-shape) at the optimal D/E. Beyond that point, distress risk raises both Kd and Ke faster than the tax shield saves.

WACC vs Leverage — Interactive Visualiser

Pecking Order Theory

Myers & Majluf (1984)

Due to information asymmetry, managers prefer internal funds first, then debt, then equity (last resort). No target D/E — structure is a residual of financing decisions over time. Explains why profitable firms use less debt (more RE available).

Market Timing Theory

Baker & Wurgler (2002)

Firms issue equity when market-to-book is high (overvalued) and buy back when undervalued. Capital structure reflects accumulated timing decisions — no target.

Agency Cost Theory

Jensen & Meckling (1976)

Debt disciplines managers (free cash flow hypothesis). But excessive debt creates underinvestment and asset substitution problems. Optimal D/E trades off these agency costs.

Quick Reference

Formula Bank — Cost of Capital

Cost of Debt

Perpetual: Kd = I / NP (before tax) Kd(AT) = [I/NP] × (1−T) Redeemable (approx): Kd(AT) = [I(1−T) + (RV−NP)/n] / [(RV+NP)/2] Exact (YTM): solve NP = Σ I(1−T)/(1+r)ᵗ + RV/(1+r)ⁿ Excel: =RATE(n, −I*(1−T), NP, −RV) Zero-coupon: Kd = (RV/Issue price)^(1/n) − 1

Cost of Preference

Irredeemable: Kp = D / NP Redeemable: Kp = [D + (RV−NP)/n] / [(RV+NP)/2] Exact: Excel: =RATE(n, −D, NP, −RV) [No tax adjustment — dividends not deductible]

Cost of Equity

DGM (constant): Ke = D₁/P₀ + g = D₀(1+g)/P₀ + g DGM (new issue): Ke = D₁/[P₀(1−f)] + g CAPM: Ke = Rƒ + β(Rm−Rƒ) Earnings-price: Ke = EPS/P₀ [or + g] Bond-yield-plus: Ke = Kd(pre-tax) + risk premium Sustainable g: g = ROE × b = ROE × (1−payout ratio) Hamada (lever): βL = βU × [1+(1−T)(D/E)] Hamada (unlever): βU = βL / [1+(1−T)(D/E)]

Cost of Retained Earnings

Kr = D₁/P₀ + g [DGM — no flotation cost] Kr = Rƒ + β(Rm−Rƒ) [CAPM — same as Ke via CAPM] Kr = Kd + risk premium [Bond-yield-plus] Note: Kr ≤ Ke (no flotation on retained earnings)

WACC

WACC = Σ Wᵢ × Kᵢ(AT) = Wₑ×Kₑ + Wp×Kp + Wd×Kd(1−T) + Wr×Kr Weights: Book value or Market value (preferred) Must sum to 1.0 MM (no tax): WACC = rA (constant) MM (with tax): WACC = rA × [1 − T×(D/V)] V_L = V_U + T×D

MCC Break Points

Break Point = Limit of cheaper source / Weight of source BP_equity = Available retained earnings / Wₑ BP_debt = Limit of lower-cost debt / Wd Each break point creates a new (higher) WACC step. Optimal budget: where MCC = IOS (IRR of marginal project)

Capital Structure (MM)

Without tax: V_L = V_U; WACC constant Ke = rA + (rA−Kd)×(D/E) With tax: V_L = V_U + T×D WACC = rA(1−T×D/V) Ke = rA + (rA−Kd)(1−T)(D/E) Trade-off: V* maximises [V_U + PV(tax shield) − PV(distress)] Optimal D/E: marginal tax benefit = marginal distress cost Pecking order: RE → Debt → New equity

Excel Quick Reference

Kd (after-tax YTM): =RATE(n, −I*(1−T), NP, −RV) Kp (exact): =RATE(n, −Div, NP, −RV) Beta from returns: =SLOPE(stock_range, mkt_range) CAPM Ke: =Rf + Beta*(Rm−Rf) WACC (SUMPRODUCT): =SUMPRODUCT(weight_array, cost_array) Sustainable g: =ROE * (1 − payout_ratio) PV of dividends: =NPV(Ke, div_range)
