Concept & Meaning

What Is the Modified Internal Rate of Return?

The Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR) is a revision of IRR that fixes its two famous flaws. Instead of assuming interim cash flows are reinvested at the project's own (often implausibly high) IRR, MIRR uses two explicit rates: a finance rate (f) to discount outflows and a reinvestment rate (rr) — usually the firm's cost of capital or a realistic market rate — to compound inflows. The result is a single, well-defined rate of return that does not depend on the sign pattern of the cash flows.

MIRR = ( Terminal Value of inflows / PV of outflows )^(1/n) − 1 where the Terminal Value compounds every positive CF to the end of the project at rr, and the PV of outflows discounts every negative CF to time 0 at f.

Why MIRR exists

Flaw 1 — Multiple IRRs
IRR can give several answers for non-conventional flows. MIRR always gives exactly one.
Flaw 2 — Reinvest at IRR
IRR assumes you can reinvest at the project's IRR. MIRR uses a realistic, stated reinvest rate.
Result
One economically sensible rate, consistent with NPV's reinvestment logic.

Teaching note: Motivate MIRR by first showing the −100/+230/−132 example where IRR gives 10% AND 20% — then ask "which is the return?" MIRR collapses both into a single sensible number. That moment sells the whole method.

MIRR in one sentence

"MIRR is the rate that grows the present value of your outflows into the future value of your inflows by the end of the project — using realistic finance and reinvestment rates."

When MIRR = IRR

If the finance rate, the reinvest rate, and the IRR all coincide (f = rr = IRR), MIRR reduces to the ordinary IRR. In practice f and rr differ from IRR, so MIRR usually differs — and is the more honest figure.
Foundations

The MIRR Formula & Derivation

Separate the cash flows into outflows (negative) and inflows (positive). Compound the inflows forward to year n at rr; discount the outflows back to time 0 at f. MIRR is the rate that equates the two.

Step 1 — Terminal Value (TV): compound all positive CFs to year n at rr: TV = Σ_{Cₜ > 0} Cₜ × (1 + rr)^(n − t) Step 2 — PV of Costs (PVC): discount all negative CFs to time 0 at f: PVC = Σ_{Cₜ < 0} |Cₜ| / (1 + f)^t Step 3 — MIRR: MIRR = ( TV / PVC )^(1/n) − 1

Worked example

Flows (₹): C₀ = −1000, C₁ = 300, C₂ = 420, C₃ = 680. f = 12%, rr = 12%, n = 3. TV = 300×1.12² + 420×1.12 + 680 = 376.3 + 470.4 + 680 = 1,526.7 PVC = 1000 (only the initial outlay is negative) MIRR = (1,526.7 / 1,000)^(1/3) − 1 = 15.2%

Why MIRR is single-valued

Compounding and discounting are monotonic transformations — they cannot create extra roots the way the raw NPV polynomial does. The ratio TV/PVC is positive and unique, so its nth root is a single real number (for any project with at least one outflow and one inflow).

Subtlety: MIRR assumes all inflows are reinvested at rr until year n, and all outflows are financed at f from time 0. This is a simplification (in reality cash flows arrive and are reinvested continuously), but it is far more defensible than reinvesting at the IRR.
Teaching note: Have students compute MIRR for the multiple-IRR example (−100, +230, −132) after this page. They will find a single 12% answer where IRR gave 10% and 20% — the "aha" that MIRR resolves the ambiguity.

Foundations

Calculating MIRR — Step by Step

The procedure is mechanical: compound the positives, discount the negatives, take the nth root.

Uneven flows — full walkthrough

Project: ICF ₹5,00,000; CFs ₹2,00,000 (Yr1), ₹2,80,000 (Yr2), ₹3,00,000 (Yr3). f = 10%, rr = 10%, n = 3. TV = 2,00,000×1.10² + 2,80,000×1.10 + 3,00,000 = 2,42,000 + 3,08,000 + 3,00,000 = 8,50,000 PVC = 5,00,000 MIRR = (8,50,000 / 5,00,000)^(1/3) − 1 = (1.70)^(0.3333) − 1 = 19.0%

Annuity short-cut

For level inflows CF for n years, starting Yr 1: TV = CF × FVIFA(rr, n) = CF × [ (1+rr)ⁿ − 1 ] / rr PVC = ICF (if only initial outlay) MIRR = ( TV / ICF )^(1/n) − 1

Common pitfalls

Forgetting that outflows after Year 0 are discounted at f (not zero) — a mid-life cleanup cost still enters PVC.
Using the wrong exponent: positive CF at year t compounds for (n − t) periods, not n.
Confusing MIRR with IRR when f ≠ rr — they differ precisely because of the two-rate assumption.
Exam tip: Always show TV and PVC explicitly before taking the root. If the question gives you f and rr separately, do NOT accidentally set them equal — that defeats the purpose of MIRR.
Teaching note: Contrast this with IRR: for the same flows, IRR solves a polynomial (possibly multiple roots); MIRR is a single deterministic computation. Stress that MIRR's "cost" is the extra inputs (two rates) — which is exactly its advantage.

MIRR checklist

1. Split CFs into inflows (+) and outflows (−).
2. Compound +CFs to Yr n at rr.
3. Discount −CFs to Yr 0 at f.
4. MIRR = (TV/PVC)^(1/n) − 1.
MIRR is expressed as a percentage, like IRR — but it is reinvestment-honest.

Reinvestment

The Reinvestment-Rate Critique — Why MIRR Exists

IRR's deepest flaw is its implicit assumption that every interim cash flow is reinvested at the project's own IRR. When a project's IRR is far above what the firm can actually earn (its WACC or a market rate), IRR overstates the true compounded return. MIRR replaces that assumption with an explicit, realistic reinvest rate.

IRR's hidden assumption: each ₹1 received early is reinvested to earn the project's IRR. If IRR = 40% but the firm can only reinvest at 12% (WACC), the IRR overstates the project's real compounded wealth. MIRR's assumption: interim inflows are reinvested at rr (a realistic rate); outflows are financed at f. This yields a single, economically sensible rate.

Proven: IRR overstates when IRR > rr

Terminal wealth under IRR assumption: |C₀| × (1+IRR)ⁿ Terminal wealth under MIRR assumption: TV = Σ Cₜ(1+rr)^{n−t} If IRR > rr → (1+IRR)ⁿ > (TV/|C₀|) → IRR terminal value > MIRR terminal value. So IRR implicitly assumes a richer reinvestment than is realistic → it overstates the return. MIRR reports the return using the realistic rr instead.

Numerical illustration

Project returns ₹1,500 at Yr 1; ICF ₹1,000; n = 5; WACC/rr = 12%. IRR = 50% (1,500/1,000 − 1). IRR terminal value at Yr 5 = 1,000 × 1.50⁵ = ₹7,593. MIRR terminal value = 1,500 × 1.12⁴ = ₹2,360 (only ₹1,500 reinvested at 12%). MIRR = (2,360 / 1,000)^(1/5) − 1 = 18.7% ≪ 50% IRR. The 50% IRR implicitly required reinvesting ₹1,500 at 50% — impossible in practice.
Takeaway: A high IRR is "penalised" by reality — its early cash flows cannot actually be reinvested at that lofty rate. MIRR bakes in a realistic reinvestment rate and therefore reports a lower, more honest return.
Teaching note: The reinvestment critique is the single best reason to prefer MIRR. Show students the "compounded value at WACC" column: IRR's number implicitly requires reinvesting at IRR, and once they grasp that, MIRR feels like a natural cure.

Reinvestment at a glance

IRR: reinvest at IRR (often unrealistically high).
MIRR: reinvest at rr (realistic, e.g. WACC).
This is the same reinvestment logic as NPV (which assumes reinvestment at WACC). MIRR is the "percentage-form" of that logic.
Reinvestment

Finance Rate vs Reinvest Rate

MIRR's two inputs are deliberately distinct because outflows and inflows are financed and reinvested in different markets.

Finance rate (f): the cost of capital used to fund the project's outflows. Discounts every negative CF to time 0. Typical proxy: the firm's borrowing rate or WACC. Reinvest rate (rr): the rate at which interim inflows can be reinvested. Compounds every positive CF to year n. Typical proxy: the firm's WACC, or the return on its next-best investment.

How the rates move MIRR

ChangeEffect on MIRRIntuition
Higher reinvest rate rrMIRR ↑Inflows compound more → richer terminal value
Lower reinvest rate rrMIRR ↓Inflows compound less
Higher finance rate fMIRR ↓Costs are dearer in PV terms
Lower finance rate fMIRR ↑Costs are cheaper

The degenerate case

If f = rr = r, then MIRR = ( TV / PVC )^(1/n) − 1 with TV = Σ Cₜ(1+r)^{n−t} (positive) and PVC = Σ |Cₜ|(1+r)^{−t} (negative). For a conventional single-outlay project this collapses to the ordinary IRR. So IRR is just MIRR with one rate forced to equal the IRR itself — which is why IRR hides its reinvestment assumption.
Best practice: Set rr = the firm's WACC (or a conservative market rate) and f = the firm's cost of debt or WACC. Never set rr = the project's IRR — that recreates IRR's flaw.
Teaching note: Have students recompute MIRR with rr = WACC, then rr = 6%, then rr = 20%. They watch MIRR climb and fall, internalising that the "return" depends on the believable reinvestment rate — not on the project's own IRR.

Choosing the rates

rr (reinvest): WACC, or the return on the firm's alternative investments.
f (finance): the borrowing rate for the funds used, or WACC.
Never: set rr = IRR — that just reproduces IRR.
Many textbooks simplify by setting f = rr = WACC. That is defensible and keeps MIRR consistent with NPV.
Special Cases

MIRR & the Multiple-IRRs Problem

IRR can yield several rates when cash flows change sign more than once. MIRR cannot — its TV/PVC ratio is positive and unique, so its nth root is a single real rate. This is MIRR's killer feature.

Worked: the classic ambiguous project

Flows (₹): C₀ = −100, C₁ = +230, C₂ = −132. (signs: −, +, − → up to 2 IRRs) IRR solves: −100 + 230/(1+r) − 132/(1+r)² = 0 → x = 1/(1+r): 132x² − 230x + 100 = 0 → x = 0.9091 or 0.8333 → IRR = 10% AND 20%. MIRR (f = 12%, rr = 12%, n = 2): TV = 230 × (1.12)^(2−1) = 230 × 1.12 = 257.6 PVC = 100 + 132/(1.12)² = 100 + 105.23 = 205.23 MIRR = (257.6 / 205.23)^(1/2) − 1 = 1.2544^0.5 − 1 = 12.0%

What this means

  • IRR gave two answers (10%, 20%) — which is "the" return? Ambiguous.
  • MIRR gave one answer (≈12%) — clear and decision-useful.
  • The single MIRR sits between the two IRRs, reflecting a realistic reinvestment rate.
Rule: When sign changes exceed one, do not use IRR. Report MIRR (or NPV). If you must quote a percentage, MIRR is the honest one.
Teaching note: After showing the two IRRs, ask students to compute NPV at r = 15%: −100 + 200 − 99.8 ≈ 0 (slightly negative). The punchline: "both IRRs are 15%? No — at 15% the project loses money." MIRR's single 12% is the sensible summary.

Why MIRR is unique

Compounding positive CFs and discounting negative CFs are monotonic — they cannot create extra roots.
TV > 0 and PVC > 0 ⇒ TV/PVC > 0 ⇒ one positive real nth root (for n ≥ 1). No multiple roots, ever.
Comparison

MIRR vs NPV — Two Sides of the Same Coin

MIRR and NPV are closer cousins than MIRR and IRR. Both value cash flows at explicit, realistic rates; both are single-valued; both agree on accept/reject for independent projects (when rr = WACC = discount rate).

DimensionNPVMIRR
Unit₹ (absolute)% (rate)
ReinvestmentAt WACCAt stated rr
UniquenessAlways oneAlways one
Multiple IRR issueNot applicableResolved

AdditiveYes (NPV(A+B)=NPV(A)+NPV(B))No

Consistency condition

With rr = WACC = discount rate, a project has NPV > 0 ⇔ MIRR > WACC. (Proof: NPV > 0 ⇒ TV > PVC×(1+WACC)ⁿ ⇒ (TV/PVC)^(1/n) > 1+WACC ⇒ MIRR > WACC.) So for independent projects, "MIRR > WACC" and "NPV > 0" are the same decision.
Takeaway: Think of MIRR as "NPV expressed as a percentage return." Use NPV for the actual decision and ranking; use MIRR when a percentage is needed for communication — especially for non-conventional cash flows where IRR misbehaves.
Teaching note: Emphasise the consistency identity. Once students see MIRR > WACC ⇔ NPV > 0 (under matching rates), they understand MIRR is not a rival to NPV but its percentage-form sibling.

Which to use when

Decision / ranking: NPV (absolute, additive, correct).
Percentage story: MIRR (reinvestment-honest, unique).
Non-conventional CFs: MIRR (or NPV), never IRR.
MIRR in Practice

When (and When Not) to Use MIRR

Strengths

✓ Unique rate for every project, including non-conventional cash flows (no multiple-IRR ambiguity).
✓ Uses realistic reinvestment and finance rates — no hidden "reinvest at IRR" assumption.
✓ Expressed as a familiar percentage, easy to compare with the cost of capital.

Weaknesses / cautions

✗ Still a percentage — it does not capture absolute value, so it can misrank mutually exclusive projects (a large low-MIRR project may beat a small high-MIRR one).
✗ Depends on two extra inputs (f and rr); the answer shifts with them. Sensitivity analysis is essential.
✗ Not additive — you cannot average MIRRs across projects.

Practical guidance

1. Make the accept/reject and ranking decision with NPV. 2. For communication, report MIRR (preferred over IRR) when cash flows are uneven or non-conventional. 3. If forced to rank mutually exclusive projects by a percentage, use incremental IRR or incremental MIRR — never standalone MIRR. 4. Always show NPV alongside MIRR.
Bottom line: MIRR is the best percentage return metric we have, but NPV remains the decision metric. MIRR ≠ a substitute for NPV on mutually exclusive choices.
Teaching note: Frame MIRR as "IRR, corrected." It should not displace NPV; it should displace IRR in communication. Students who remember that one sentence avoid every MIRR pitfall.

MIRR vs the family

NPV: primary decision metric (₹).
MIRR: best % metric (reinvestment-honest).
IRR: % metric, flawed (use only if sign pattern is conventional and you disclose reinvestment).
For a conventional project, MIRR ≈ IRR when rr and f are near WACC; the gap widens as the IRR departs from realistic reinvestment rates.
Special Cases

Special Cases & Edge Conditions

1 · All inflows after Year 0 (no intermediate outflows)

This is the normal case: one outflow at t=0, inflows thereafter. PVC = |C₀|; TV = Σ Cₜ(1+rr)^{n−t}. MIRR is well-defined.

2 · All outflows (no positive CF)

No inflows to reinvest ⇒ TV = 0 ⇒ MIRR = (0 / PVC)^(1/n) − 1 = −100% (or undefined). The project destroys value; reject on NPV. MIRR correctly signals catastrophe.

3 · Sign change but TV = PVC

If TV = PVC exactly, MIRR = 0% — the project breaks even in compounded terms. Rare, but a clean signal.

4 · Scale and ranking

Like IRR, MIRR is a ratio-style rate and can misrank mutually exclusive projects. A ₹1,000 project at MIRR 25% may be worth less than a ₹10,00,000 project at MIRR 18%. Rank by NPV, or use incremental MIRR.

5 · Sensitivity to the rates

MIRR(rr=6%) vs MIRR(rr=12%) vs MIRR(rr=18%) The spread can be several percentage points. Always report the rr and f used, and stress-test.
Summary: MIRR is robust to the sign-pattern problems that break IRR, but it remains a percentage and inherits the scale/ranking limitations of all rate metrics. Pair it with NPV.
Teaching note: The "all outflows" case is a neat way to show MIRR's honesty: where IRR might return "no solution" or a confusing number, MIRR calmly returns −100% (total loss). It never silently misleads.

Edge-case checklist

Normal: outflow t=0, inflows later → MIRR fine.
No inflows: TV=0 → MIRR = −100% (reject).
Mutually exclusive: rank by NPV, not MIRR.
Always state f and rr when you quote a MIRR — the number is meaningless without them.

Comprehensive Worked Examples

Example 1 — Basic MIRR

ICF ₹1,000; CFs ₹300, ₹420, ₹680; f = rr = 12%; n = 3.

TV = 300×1.12² + 420×1.12 + 680 = 376.3+470.4+680 = 1,526.7. PVC = 1,000. MIRR = (1,526.7/1,000)^(1/3) − 1 = 15.2%.

Example 2 — MIRR with annuity

ICF ₹5,00,000; ₹2,00,000/yr for 3 yrs; f = rr = 10%; n = 3.

TV = 2,00,000×FVIFA(10%,3) = 2,00,000×3.31 = 6,62,000. PVC = 5,00,000. MIRR = (6,62,000/5,00,000)^(1/3) − 1 = (1.324)^0.333 − 1 = 9.8%.

Example 3 — MIRR < IRR

Same as Ex.1. IRR ≈ 21% (solve −1000+300/(1+r)+420/(1+r)²+680/(1+r)³=0). MIRR = 15.2%.

IRR ≈ 21% (reinvest at 21% assumed) but MIRR = 15.2% (reinvest at 12%). Because IRR > rr, IRR overstates. MIRR is the honest figure.

Example 4 — Multiple IRRs resolved

Flows −100, +230, −132; f = rr = 12%; n = 2. (IRR = 10% and 20%.)

TV = 230×1.12 = 257.6. PVC = 100 + 132/1.2544 = 205.23. MIRR = (257.6/205.23)^0.5 − 1 = 12.0%. One answer where IRR gave two.

Example 5 — Different f and rr

ICF ₹1,000; CFs ₹400, ₹500, ₹600; f = 10%, rr = 16%; n = 3.

TV = 400×1.16² + 500×1.16 + 600 = 538.2+580+600 = 1,718.2. PVC = 1,000. MIRR = (1,718.2/1,000)^(1/3) − 1 = 19.8%. Higher rr lifts MIRR vs the f=rr=12% case.

Example 6 — Reinvestment critique

ICF ₹1,000; ₹1,500 at Yr1; n = 5; rr = 12%. IRR = 50%.

TV = 1,500×1.12⁴ = 2,360. PVC = 1,000. MIRR = (2,360/1,000)^(1/5) − 1 = 18.7% ≪ 50% IRR. The 50% IRR implied reinvesting at 50% — impossible; MIRR is honest.

Example 7 — Consistency with NPV

Same as Ex.1, WACC = 12%. Show MIRR > WACC ⇔ NPV > 0.

NPV = 300/1.12 + 420/1.12² + 680/1.12³ − 1000 = 267.9+334.8+484.2−1000 = +₹87.0 > 0. MIRR 15.2% > WACC 12%. Consistent: NPV>0 ⇔ MIRR>WACC.

Example 8 — Scale note

Project A: ICF ₹1,000, one CF ₹1,500 Yr1 (IRR 50%, MIRR 18.7%). Project B: ICF ₹10,000, one CF ₹12,000 Yr1 (IRR 20%, MIRR ~15%). Mutually exclusive?

A has higher MIRR; B has higher NPV (₹909 vs ₹364 at 12%). For mutually exclusive projects, choose B (NPV). MIRR, like IRR, can misrank on scale — use NPV.
