Fundamentals

Meaning and Need for ARR

The Accounting Rate of Return (ARR, also called the Average Rate of Return or Return on Investment) measures the expected accounting profitability of a project as a percentage: the average annual profit after tax, over the project's life, divided by the average investment locked in the asset.

Definition: ARR is the ratio of the average expected profit after tax (PAT) to the average investment in the project, expressed as a percentage. Unlike payback, NPV or IRR, it is built from accounting profits — the same figures managers already report — rather than from cash flows.

Its appeal is direct: management thinks in terms of return-on-investment percentages ("this machine should earn 20% a year on its book value"), and ARR produces exactly that number from readily available projected income statements.

Forecast PAT each year→ Compute average PAT→ Compute average investment→ ARR = avg PAT ÷ avg investment→ Compare with target
Teaching note: Anchor with a familiar ratio: ARR is essentially a projected ROCE/ROI for the asset. Students then see both its appeal (consistent with how performance is measured post-investment) and its flaw (book profits and book values are accounting conventions, not cash).

The Method — Step by Step

Annual depreciation (straight line) = (Cost − Salvage value) ÷ Life (years) Average annual profit (PAT) = Σ PAT over life ÷ Number of years Average investment = (Cost + Salvage value) ÷ 2 (= average book value over the life) ARR = (Average annual profit ÷ Average investment) × 100

Indian/textbook convention uses average investment = (initial cost + salvage)/2 — the average book value over the asset's life. Always state which basis a percentage uses; the accept/reject signal can differ.
PAT figures are already after depreciation and tax. Do not subtract depreciation again — that double-counts it.

The Decision Rule

ARR ≥ Target (hurdle) rate → ACCEPT ARR < Target (hurdle) rate → REJECT Ranking projects: higher ARR is preferred.

Ranking conflict warning: ARR ranks by a percentage return, not by absolute value. A small high-percentage project can outrank a large project that adds far more rupees of profit — ARR ignores scale just as IRR can.

Why the hurdle matters

The target ARR is usually set with reference to the firm's cost of capital and the sector's normal accounting return. It is a policy threshold, not a market-determined rate.
Mutual exclusivity: accept the project with the highest ARR provided it clears the hurdle — but always cross-check against NPV, since ARR ignores timing.
Analysis

Strengths and Weaknesses

Advantages

Simple and familiar. Built from projected income statements; expressed as a percentage managers already use (ROI/ROCE style).
Considers the whole life. Unlike payback, it uses profits from every year of the project, including the tail.
Easy comparison. Percentage returns are readily compared across projects and with the firm's target return.
Links to reported performance. Post-investment, divisional ROI is measured the same way, so the appraisal yardstick matches the evaluation yardstick.

Weaknesses

Ignores the time value of money. A rupee of Year-5 profit counts the same as a rupee of Year-1 profit; no discounting.
Uses accounting profit, not cash flow. Depreciation conventions and non-cash charges drive the number; two depreciation methods give two ARRs for the same project.
Ignores the timing of profits. Projects with the same average PAT but very different timing rank equally, although value differs.
Arbitrary hurdle & denominator. Target return is judgement-based; average-vs-initial investment conventions change the result.

Where it fits

Quick profitability screen for small, replacement-type projects where full DCF feels disproportionate.
Reporting alignment: useful where managers are evaluated on book ROI and want appraisal to mirror that metric.
Never decide solely on ARR. It is not NPV-consistent: it can accept negative-NPV projects (far-dated profits) or reject value-adding ones (heavy early depreciation). Use NPV/IRR to confirm.
Teaching note: Demonstrate the timing flaw with two projects having identical average PAT but back-loaded vs front-loaded profits — ARR ties them, NPV strongly prefers the front-loaded one. This shows why "average percentage" cannot substitute for discounted cash flow.

The core divide: value is created by cash arriving early. ARR measures neither — it measures accrual-profit averaged across years. That makes it a convenient accounting summary, never a substitute for DCF.

Same project, different verdicts?

A project with large early depreciation (low early PAT) can show a modest ARR yet have strong early cash flows and positive NPV — ARR may reject it wrongly.
Conversely, a project with steady book profits far in the future can clear the ARR hurdle while its discounted cash flows yield negative NPV.
Rule of thumb: compute ARR for the accounting summary and stakeholder reporting; make the accept/reject call on NPV (with IRR/PI as cross-checks).

Practice

Comprehensive Worked Examples

Example 1 — Standard ARR

A machine costs ₹5,00,000 with ₹50,000 salvage after 5 years. Expected PAT (after depreciation & tax): ₹40,000 · ₹60,000 · ₹80,000 · ₹50,000 · ₹30,000. Target ARR 15%. Decide.

Depreciation = (5,00,000 − 50,000)/5 = ₹90,000 p.a. Average PAT = (40+60+80+50+30)/5 = ₹52,000. Average investment = (5,00,000+50,000)/2 = ₹2,75,000.

ARR = 52,000/2,75,000 = 18.91% > 15% → accept. (On the initial-cost basis: 52,000/5,00,000 = 10.40% — state the basis.)

Example 2 — High-return project

Equipment costs ₹10,00,000 with zero salvage over 4 years. Expected PAT: ₹1,00,000 · ₹1,50,000 · ₹1,80,000 · ₹1,20,000. Target 15%. Find ARR.

Average PAT = (1,00,000+1,50,000+1,80,000+1,20,000)/4 = ₹1,37,500. Average investment = (10,00,000+0)/2 = ₹5,00,000.

ARR = 1,37,500/5,00,000 = 27.50% > 15% → accept; rank above the reference machine.

Example 3 — Below-hurdle rejection

An asset costs ₹8,00,000 with ₹40,000 salvage after 4 years. Expected PAT: ₹20,000 · ₹30,000 · ₹35,000 · ₹25,000. Target 15%. Decide.

Average PAT = (20,000+30,000+35,000+25,000)/4 = ₹27,500. Average investment = (8,00,000+40,000)/2 = ₹4,20,000.

ARR = 27,500/4,20,000 = 6.55% < 15% → reject.

Example 4 — Ranking vs scale

Project B (Example 2) offers ARR 27.5% on ₹10L; a small Project D offers ARR 30% on ₹1,00,000 (average PAT ₹15,000). Rank by ARR and comment on NPV logic.

ARR ranking: D (30%) above B (27.5%). But D earns only ₹15,000/yr on average while B earns ₹1,37,500/yr — nine times the rupee profit at a marginally lower percentage.

ARR ignores scale: under capital rationing use NPV or the Profitability Index to rank; ARR's percentage ranking is not wealth-maximising. Accept B first if funds allow both.

Formula Bank — ARR

Straight-line depreciation: D = (Cost − Salvage) ÷ Life Average annual profit: Avg PAT = (Σ PAT over life) ÷ n Average investment: Avg I = (Cost + Salvage) ÷ 2 ARR = (Avg PAT ÷ Avg I) × 100 Variant: ARR(on cost) = Avg PAT ÷ Cost × 100

Decision rules

ARR ≥ target (hurdle) rate → ACCEPT ARR < target rate → REJECT Mutually exclusive: highest ARR above the hurdle (verify with NPV).
ARR is not NPV-consistent: it ignores cash timing, uses accounting profit, and depends on depreciation policy. Report it as an accounting summary; decide with DCF.
